# Stage 1A: Vision (six pillars)

## Purpose

A vision does two jobs: orientation (points you the right way) and energization (fuels progress). The program quotes Seneca: a ship does not know which way to sail until it knows its port. Without a destination people work hard and zigzag back near where they started. The program also says synthesizer freedom is a non-normal outcome, so it takes non-normal inputs, including obsession.

The worksheet section is "Vision: Why am I here". Instructions on the left, answers on the right. Dump all thoughts first, then edit each pillar down to at most three lines.

## The six pillars and the quality bar for each

1. Current problems. What is holding the user back from scaling, what they struggle with, what they wish were different. Confront these face on.
   - Bar: specific and felt. At least one problem about leads or clients, one about time or energy, one about money or pricing. No blame of "the algorithm" or "the market" without a concrete example.
   - Probes: "What happened the last time this went wrong?" "What do you keep putting off?" "Where do you lose the most hours for the least return?"
2. Current income. An honest average of the last six months, not the best month. The program says brutally honest, not optimistic.
   - Bar: one number or a range with the typical value, and the source (invoices, bank, Stripe). If the user is rounding up, ask for the median month.
3. Consequences. What happens if nothing changes. The cost of staying put. Who is hurt if the user fails.
   - Bar: names people and costs (health, family, time, money, the clients who never get helped). The program's own example includes "if I fail, my clients fail".
   - Probes: "In 12 months, if nothing changes, what is your week like?" "Who depends on you?" "Who never gets your help because you are capped?"
4. Desired situation. The world the user wishes existed. Dream big, paint it. Daydreaming the future is part of the method because feeling it makes it tangible.
   - Bar: concrete scenes (a typical Tuesday, where they live, how many hours they work, what they spend time on), not adjectives like "freedom".
   - Probes: "Describe a Tuesday morning in that life." "What are you doing that you cannot do now?" "What would you stop doing?"
5. Desired income. One specific dollar amount per month, so the user can tell whether they have hit it. The program's own worked example staged it: $10K per month, then $30K, then $100K. Staging is fine. Each stage needs a number and a date.
   - Bar: a number, a date, and a plausible path (for example "$X per month = N clients at $Y"). If the number is far above what the current offer or capacity could produce, say so and ask for a staged version.
6. Mission. The positive impact beyond income. Write it even if it feels unclear or cringy. Missions tend to mirror the user's own life: they want to change people's lives the way theirs changed.
   - Bar: names who is helped and what changes for them, in plain words. Not a slogan.
   - Probes: "Whose life do you want to change?" "What did someone do for you that changed your path?"

## Writing rules from the program

- Execution, not perfection. The vision evolves. Get it good, take it to market, get feedback, iterate.
- Do not lie to yourself. Write what the user wants, ignoring what friends, parents or partners think they should want.
- Specific monthly income goals orient best.
- Clarity beats complexity. No essays. Use pain as fuel and boil it down.

## Pressure test before locking

- Is the current income an honest six-month average?
- Does the desired income have a number and a date?
- Does the mission connect to the pain in "current problems" (the program's pattern is that the mission mirrors the user's own change)?
- Does the desired situation describe a day, not a feeling?
- Do the consequences name a person or a cost?
- Is any pillar longer than three lines? Compress.

## Gap check that connects to later stages

Say it out loud if you see these:
- Current income is stable but the desired income needs 10x the clients. That points to a premium offer and price work in 1D.
- Consequences mention burnout or time. That affects delivery model choice in 1D and 2A.
- Mission is broad ("help everyone") while proof is narrow. That affects 1C.
