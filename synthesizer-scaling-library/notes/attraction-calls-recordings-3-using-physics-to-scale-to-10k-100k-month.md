# Using Physics To Scale To $10k - $100k/month
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: 1,610 transcript lines
## One-paragraph summary
This call prepares members for a 90-day Skool growth challenge called The 100. The speaker uses three physics-themed analogies to explain business execution: move quickly to buy back time, use one trusted source or mentor to compress learning, and remove the personal stress that consumes attention. The practical point is to follow a small set of proven actions with focus, prioritize product-market fit and traffic, and reduce distractions rather than continually adding platforms or systems. Q&A covers launch timing, qualifying calls, cohorts, discovery, and when a business owner should resist building more and start selling.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **Goals as effects, actions as causes:** A goal is a desired future effect. If it is possible and others have achieved it, actions can produce it. The call frames the Scaling roadmap as a concise set of causes to execute consistently.
- **Speed runner mindset:** Identify the desired outcome and focus intensely on actions that can compress the timeline. The relativity analogy motivates urgency; it is not presented as a detailed physics lesson.
- **One Source Rule / one source “wormhole”:** Learn from someone who has achieved the outcome you want and implement one source fully before adding other advice. The speaker links this focus to faster progress. He consumed Sam Ovens' content for two years, completed the program twice, and reviewed core recordings. Mixing puzzle sets represents competing strategies.
- **Underlying radiation:** Ongoing stress, unresolved responsibilities, relationship tension, poor sleep, clutter, or other personal concerns can consume attention needed for business. Identify the most persistent distraction and resolve or reduce it. The speaker estimates that many people spend only 10% to 20% of their mental capacity on business because other concerns take up the rest. This is a coaching metaphor, not a measured diagnostic.
- **GPS framework:** G is Goal, P is Problem, S is Solution. Define the goal, identify the problem that blocks it, then choose an action that addresses that problem. The speaker presents this as a practical way to choose work and ask useful questions.
- **Relative productivity:** Choose work by the goal and current constraint, not by whether it is generally productive. When sales are the constraint, booking and closing calls outrank journaling, meditation, or complex funnels.
- **Attract, Convert, Deliver:** Attract builds attention through content, networking, a free group, and outreach. Convert includes conversations and sales calls. Deliver includes improving the program and customer success. The speaker describes this as the core operating loop; operations such as hiring or payment administration become more relevant as a team grows.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. **Prepare for a 90-day push.** Everyone could attend The 100 calls and use its resources, but only ranked participants counted officially. Ask Aidan if status is unclear. Before kickoff, choose one distraction to address during the next seven days.
2. **Clear focus blockers.** Write down the biggest ongoing stressor. Examples included a messy home, a partner who resents work hours, a friend whose judgment creates anxiety, an unresolved commitment, or irregular sleep. Work on the main issue by the start of the challenge. The speaker emphasized persistent background concerns over a discrete family visit; personal decisions about work and family remain individual.
3. **Communicate a temporary work sprint.** Tell family or partners the duration, purpose, expected change in work hours, and household benefit. Acknowledge the impact; this is a personal choice, not a rule to deprioritize family.
4. **Use the daily GPS sequence.** In the morning, review the goal and the current action steps. Identify the bottleneck, work on the highest-leverage solution first, remove distractions, finish it, then move to the next task. During The 100, members were to follow the weekly cohort actions before adding unrelated tasks.
5. **Simplify the task list.** List business tasks from highest leverage to lowest and cut the bottom items. The speaker specifically advises against adding a new platform or complicated setup when a simpler action would support the goal.
6. **Run the core business loop.** Create content on a primary platform, use Skool networking where the audience is present, maintain a free Skool group where appropriate, reach out to people who join or engage, hold sales calls, improve the offer or program, and follow up with customers. A business can reach $50,000 per month without a sales rep, appointment setter, or customer success manager, according to the speaker; he said those hires may come later.
7. **Research-call options.** A research call can gather message-resonance data and test whether the niche, problem, and offer fit. If using a two-call process, add questions about urgency and ability to invest, then invite a qualified prospect to a separate sales call. If the conversation is purely research, keep it research rather than forcing a pitch.
8. **Build delivery before a cohort.** Start with a few one-to-one clients to learn what works, then convert the process into a cohort. The speaker cites five one-to-one customers before turning Scaling into a cohort, and says around 10 clients is a reasonable point to consider the transition. One-to-one customers can still join cohort calls for group accountability while receiving additional one-to-one sessions.
9. **Make decisions using enough data.** A close rate above 20% was offered as a rough sign of product-market fit. If below 20%, investigate whether prospects want the problem solved and trust the creator's proof. Experiment with offers and custom plans in conversations. This is a heuristic, not a guaranteed diagnostic.
10. **Example launch timing.** Revenue tracking began April 1; The 100 kickoff and prep were April 3, attract started April 11, and convert training began April 18, followed by delivery. Members could launch early, though the speaker recommended following the cohort for feedback.

## Examples and case studies (member or company, what they did, the numbers and result; only what the transcript says)
- **Sam Ovens program:** The host said he was making about $2,000 per month from low-ticket products before joining. After learning high-ticket sales and business structure, he reached $30,000 monthly and later $100,000 monthly. He attributes the change to the program and focused implementation.
- **The 100:** The original 90-day competition was meant to gather growth proof for a planned $100 million book launch. Skool later shifted its emphasis toward community and passion. The updated challenge added launch training before evergreen attraction.
- **Partnership development:** A member asked about finding a “ghost” operator. The host advised against rushing into a partnership. His example with Aidan began with a small Twitter writing task, then expanded into community moderation and further responsibilities after repeated good performance.
- **Cohort design:** Scaling began with five one-to-one clients, used to test the process before cohort delivery. The speaker said a member moving toward 10 clients was at a suitable transition point.
- **Sales-system quality:** A dialer increased calls but lowered call quality and close rate. Reviewing booking forms live, asking about weak answers, selectively dialing prospects, and reviewing setter recordings improved qualification. A 50% triage-to-sales-call rate was described as acceptable if prospects were better qualified.
- **A member's offer:** A participant had nine job-seeking clients, with one job placement after about three months, despite a four-to-five-month promise. The host said not to change the niche yet; examine which actions separate the successful client, improve delivery, and avoid promising a fixed result or time frame unless it can be consistently supported.
## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not confuse consuming more advice with progress. Mixing sources can consume time that should go to implementation.
- Do not add low-leverage tasks simply because they are popular productivity habits or complex software systems. Choose by the current goal and bottleneck.
- Do not rush into an equity or operating partnership with someone you have not tested. Start with a small task and increase responsibility based on performance; the speaker said he had seen partnerships fail more often than succeed.
- Do not optimize for call volume at the expense of qualified prospects. Review unqualified calls, improve the setter questions, and add appropriate friction.
- Do not change a niche as the first response to slow outcomes. Improve delivery, assess client fit, and give a process time to work.
- Do not treat the physics analogies as scientific proof of the business claims. The speaker explicitly introduced physics as an analogy.
- The VAT discussion was uncertain and time-specific. The speaker explicitly said it was not legal advice and to follow the law; do not treat speculation about country settings as tax guidance.
