# Onboarding Training
Type: lesson   Stage: Launch Deliver   Length/size: 38:34 video transcript plus onboarding template notes
## One-paragraph summary
Onboarding is the period after payment and before the program begins. Use it to affirm the client's decision, orient them to the mission and outcome, establish the identity and responsibilities that support results, explain the roadmap, provide proof, clarify expectations, and get an immediate first action. A welcome video is the recommended reusable format for group or one-to-many programs; one-to-one providers can use a customized onboarding call and document. Keep the delivery simple, energetic, and focused on the principles rather than production polish.

## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)

### Successful Onboarding Checklist
The source gives eight pillars to cover:
1. **Validation:** Affirm the purchase decision and celebrate that the client earned a spot, especially if places are limited.
2. **Vision:** Explain the larger mission and why the client is here. Share the creator's mission statement from the Business Backbone.
3. **Identity:** Describe the behaviors of the person likely to get results. Synthesizer's example is an action taker who solves problems, shares solutions, works through difficult periods, and keeps showing up.
4. **Outcome:** Restate the tangible destination. Connect the creator's former struggles and current results to the client's desired transformation, so the client can see why the creator may be able to guide them.
5. **Clarity:** Present the program phases and explain how they lead toward the outcome.
6. **Confidence:** Share names, numbers, wins, testimonials, research, or other proof that answers why the client should listen. If the creator lacks direct proof for a topic, the lesson suggests citing credible external evidence, such as research or a trustworthy quote.
7. **Expectations:** State what clients can expect from the provider and what the provider expects clients to do. The relationship requires both sides to participate and hold each other accountable.
8. **Momentum:** Explain what happens next and assign a small first step. The action should be smaller than a major quick win, but give the client an initial investment of time and effort.

### Onboarding deliverables by format
- **One-to-one:** Deliver the program through a weekly call, where the provider covers needed knowledge and actions. The provider may give a personal number for support, with an availability description such as 24-hour support Monday to Friday. This means clients may message at any time, not that the provider answers around the clock.
- **One-to-many:** Use a weekly group call, prerecorded training drops for the modules, asynchronous community support, and one-to-one support if needed. The instructor says to emphasize community support and make direct-message support less central because asynchronous answers can help more people.

### Welcome video slide flow
Use the program name and branding, then welcome and validate the client's decision. State the outcome and vision, explain why the creator is credible through a before-and-after story, present the mission, show the program roadmap with a short explanation and proof for each phase, list deliverables and support channels, clarify expectations, summarize the journey, and finish with a commitment or small action. The slides are prompts for spoken explanation, not a script to read word for word.

### One-to-one onboarding call flow
Customize the conversation to the client. Set the call purpose and expectations, ask what the client expects and what would make the work valuable, create or confirm a one-sentence mission/outcome together, review relevant foundations (the example uses a SWOT analysis), present the phases, agree on next steps, and ask follow-up questions to gather anything still needed. Share a copy of the customized onboarding document after the call.

## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Build a welcome video even if the program has not started. Use the interval between selling and delivery to prepare clients and build the program. The instructor says this also gives time to plan an engaging kickoff.
2. Duplicate the Canva slide template or create a similar presentation in another tool. Add the program name, logo, and colors. If changing brand colors in Canva, use the Brand function to apply them across the presentation.
3. Copy relevant material already created in the Business Backbone: the creator's past situation, problems, pains, desires, transformation, current results, and mission. Edit as needed; the template is not a requirement to list everything or use one slide per point.
4. Insert the program phases from the previous lesson. For each phase, state what the client is moving from and toward, then add appropriate proof. Examples include a named testimonial, result, or a credible source. If using AI to locate a quote, verify that it is real and do not use fabricated citations.
5. Describe deliverables and support plainly. Explain the normal path for help; the Synthesizer example says to go through the program, attend a call if stuck, post in the community if still stuck, search for an existing answer, and then use a personal message as an escalation.
6. State honest expectations, including the client's work and any realistic result timeline. Do not promise an outcome in a week if the work may take six months. Do not imply the provider will do everything if the client must act.
7. End with a small commitment, such as asking the client to say or write that they commit to following the program. The source suggests doing this aloud on a call, in chat, by message, or as a comment on a pinned post.
8. Keep slide text brief and speak to the points naturally. Record with Loom or OBS/native screen recording, then upload the welcome video to the classroom. The instructor's template-fill and video task should take under an hour; the Synthesizer example was 35 minutes long, or 18 minutes at double speed.
9. For one-to-one work, duplicate the Google Doc template for each client, fill it in from the discovery/sales call and the client's answers, present it live while sharing the screen, then send the client a copy.

## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Provider, opening a group welcome video:** "You made it. You're in the right place. Congrats, and welcome." Use after payment to affirm the decision and celebrate limited access where relevant.
- **Provider, connecting personal story to mission:** "And now it's my mission to help other [niche] get [dream outcome], too." Fill the brackets with the provider's audience and outcome. The example follows a story of prior struggle, the change that produced results, and the mission to help others.
- **Provider, one-to-one onboarding:** "What do you expect from working together?" Ask after explaining the purpose of the call, then record the client's words.
- **Provider, one-to-one onboarding:** "What would make this incredible for you?" Use to learn the client's criteria for a successful engagement.
- **Provider, co-writing a mission:** "What's your mission? If you had to put it in one sentence, what's your mission for this program?" Follow with a question about where the client wants to be at the end, then help them write a concise statement.
- **Provider, closing onboarding:** "I commit to following the program." Ask the client to say or write this, or adapt the wording to the program's commitment, as the final momentum step.
- **Provider, clarifying support escalation:** Go through the program first, attend a call if stuck, post in the community if still stuck, search for an existing answer, then send a personal message if needed. This is the sequence described for the Synthesizer community.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not leave clients uncertain after payment about what happens next. Weak onboarding can contribute to refunds, churn, missed starts, and failure to act.
- Do not promise a faster result than the work supports or conceal the work clients must do.
- Do not imply 24-hour support means the provider responds 24/7.
- Do not overcomplicate deliverables. Start with the simple support and delivery mechanism, then add what is needed.
- Do not make one-to-one DMs the default support channel in a group offer. The instructor prefers asynchronous community support, with one-to-one support reserved for need.
- Do not read the slides verbatim. Keep only short prompts on screen and riff from understanding.
- Do not spend time on elaborate design, transitions, camera setup, or a perfect backdrop. The example welcome video was filmed without showing a face.
- Do not invent proof. If using AI to find supporting quotations, check the source and authenticity.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this after a client pays and before delivery begins, especially when the client may be unsure what happens next, when a cohort has a start date, or when the provider wants a repeatable welcome experience. Ask whether delivery is one-to-one or one-to-many, when the program starts, what outcome was promised, what evidence supports the provider's claims, which phases the client will follow, what support is actually included, and what work the client must do. For one-to-one onboarding, ask what the client expects and what would make the engagement valuable, then tailor the document and conversation to their answers.
