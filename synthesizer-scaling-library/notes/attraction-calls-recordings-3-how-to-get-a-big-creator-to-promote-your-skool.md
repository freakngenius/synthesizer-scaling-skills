# How to get a big creator to promote your Skool
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: 1,801 lines, 14,971 words in source file
## One-paragraph summary
This call explains how joint ventures can pair a person with audience access and trust with a person who has a strong offer and delivery capacity. The instructor recommends building relationships and solving a potential partner's problems before pitching, then presenting a low-effort promotion arrangement that protects the partner's reputation. The call also covers staged delegation, hiring and commission benchmarks, staying focused on one taught business model, and becoming a trusted “star student” without treating a paid program as a transaction. Its practical default remains outreach, networking, and content, while partnerships develop over time.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **Joint-venture fit:** Pair a trusted audience source who lacks time or a monetization vehicle with an operator who has an offer and delivery capacity but needs traffic. The creator and operator are common roles. A good JV solves a problem for both parties.
- **Relationship-first JV process:** Define the niche; identify people with its attention and trust; join their communities and follow their content; help with their goals; wait for a visible need or opening; then propose a simple promotion arrangement. The partner contributes a few posts or a webinar; you handle sales and delivery. Share customer wins and explain how complaints or refunds will be handled to protect their reputation.
- **Progressive responsibility:** Start with a small task. If the person does it well, add responsibility and compensation. Do not declare a partnership or allocate ownership before working together.
- **Hiring order and prioritization:** Delegate appointment setting first, then sales when call volume consumes meaningful owner time, then customer success, and later miscellaneous work to a generalist. List tasks, weekly hours, and how easily each can be replaced (the example uses 1-to-5 scores). Delegate high-hour, low-difficulty tasks first. Video editing or ghostwriting can help but is optional.
- **Three hiring channels:** Recruit inside your community, post in other relevant communities, and audit your network for candidates or referrals.
- **One-model program design:** Teach one coherent operating model and only the training needed to execute it. A library of every requested topic can overwhelm members and obstruct implementation. Shared processes also make KPIs and hiring easier to compare.
- **Star student:** Get results, participate, and share specific testimonials or lessons. A mentor may promote a standout student, creating credibility and access for the student's later business. Do not join a paid program only to extract a JV.
- **Daily attraction fundamentals:** Outreach, networking, and content are repeatable activities. JVs can grow a business quickly, but usually develop through relationships over time.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. **Work out the call requirement:** Use the “Math to $10k/$100k a month” sheet or calculator. Enter goal and price, then close rate and show rate to estimate customers, calls taken, and calls booked.
2. **Create the delegation sheet:** In Google Sheets, list each task, weekly hours, and replaceability score. The speaker used appointment setting as relatively easy (score 1) and community responses as harder (score 3). Start with tasks that take time and are easiest to transfer.
3. **Source a hire:** Ask in your community, post in other communities that attract the role, and conduct a network audit. A hiring post should explain the role, compensation, lead volume, and expected earnings.
4. **Train a setter:** Use a 30-day trial and review messages daily at day's end. Show what you would send instead and why. If the setter is not booking consistently after 30 days, find another candidate.
5. **Limit credentials:** Do not casually share full social or platform passwords. The transcript mentions Fiduci for delegated Skool DMs without sharing the login; the instructor did not know the Instagram equivalent.
6. **Separate setting from closing:** Setters book calls. Closers handle calls and warm follow-up with people who previously expressed interest. Add a sales rep once the owner consistently has more than roughly three to four calls per day or calls consume a meaningful part of the day.
7. **Pitch once the partner signals need:** Explain the small action required of them, such as a few posts or a joint webinar; you handle sales and delivery, and they earn a share. Show results and explain refunds or support so their audience is safe.
8. **Find audience overlap:** List where the niche spends attention: social accounts, YouTube, podcasts, newsletters, Skool groups, or associations. If you have the offer but need traffic, build the relationship inside the traffic-holder's ecosystem.
9. **Test content without discarding winners:** Repeat what works and experiment when it stops. The suggested allocation is 80% proven content and 20% tests.
10. **Improve YouTube conversion:** Use YouTube Studio's bulk editor to add a CTA in the first description line on videos still receiving views. Say who the community is for and the outcome. The speaker preferred image/GIF community posts using the thumbnail and link over video-format community posts.
11. **Keep one paid-program model:** Connect lessons and calls to the method members are meant to follow. Scaling teaches high-ticket rather than adding a low-ticket track.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not assign 40% or 50% ownership to a new collaborator before testing the working relationship. Start with a small task and expand responsibility based on performance.
- Do not approach a creator with an immediate pitch before understanding their needs and establishing trust.
- Do not risk the partner's reputation by sending referred people into poor delivery. Share evidence and explain how dissatisfied customers will be handled.
- Do not give an employee broad account credentials without considering the downside. Use limited access where available.
- Do not hire a closer just because you dislike calls; wait until call volume occupies meaningful time.
- Do not build a paid curriculum as an unbounded library of every requested topic. Maintain a coherent model.
- Do not join a high-ticket program just to extract a JV or promotion. Testimonials and participation should be based on real results.
- Do not let future partnership opportunities replace today's outreach, networking, and content.
- Do not overcomplicate content testing. Repeat what works; allocate a minority of effort to experiments.
## Numbers and benchmarks (conversion rates, call counts, prices, targets, time frames, with the context for each)
- JV share: 20% to 50%; about 30% was described as more standard for high-ticket. Use the lower end when a sales rep also needs payment; 50/50 was cited for an automated arrangement.
- Commission: setter 5%; sales representative 10% to 15%. The speaker suggested 15% when lead volume is low and 10% when high.
- Setter trial: 30 days with daily feedback; look for consistent bookings by the end.
- Sales-rep threshold: usually more than three to four calls daily, when calls take significant owner time.
- Staffing estimates: one setter and one sales rep could support $100,000 to $150,000 monthly; one sales rep could reach $300,000 to $500,000 depending on the business. These are instructor estimates, not guarantees.
- Content mix: 80% known winners, 20% experimentation.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use when an educator needs a traffic source, a potential JV partner has trusted access to the target audience, or the owner is ready to delegate repetitive work. Ask what the offer delivers, what proof and customer wins exist, who already reaches the audience, what problem the partner has, and what work the user can take on. For hiring, ask current call volume, weekly time by task, which tasks are repeatable, what candidate access can be safely granted, and what the user can coach daily. For creator promotion, clarify the partner's desired outcome, the referral economics, who sells and delivers, and what happens if a referred customer is unhappy.
