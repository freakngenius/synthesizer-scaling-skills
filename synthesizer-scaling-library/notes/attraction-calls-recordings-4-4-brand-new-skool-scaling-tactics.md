# 4 Brand NEW Skool Scaling Tactics
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: Not stated in source (transcript)
## One-paragraph summary
The call reports several experiments in attraction and delivery: a hybrid cohort model, evergreen onboarding over-delivers, a repeatable “Skool hotline” content format, high-ticket recurring payments, a cold-to-sold video, and paid ads. Most are early tests, not validated prescriptions. The speaker contrasts repeating actions that have worked with risky experimentation, and advises most members to use the program's proven approach while the team gathers data. The durable basics are to keep the funnel congruent, speak to a clearly qualified avatar, make booking calls the priority, and increase the volume of content and outreach before over-optimizing minor details.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- Repeat successful actions: Repeat posts, sales-call elements, and acquisition methods that work. The weekly cohort plan was an experiment; most members were advised to wait for results rather than copy an unproven model.
- Value equation: Value depends on desired outcome and perceived likelihood, and rises when time delay and effort fall. The team reasoned that a quick backbone review and launch would reduce time to results.
- Evergreen over-deliver plus micro-cohorts: Preserve support in evergreen lessons while allowing continuous enrollment. New members join the next weekly start; classroom videos provide over-delivers and calls provide structure.
- High-ticket MRR: Combine the larger revenue per customer associated with high-ticket services with recurring monthly payments. One option described was a more intensive first phase at a higher monthly price, followed by a lower continuity price once the initial cohort, course, or setup work is complete. A paid-in-full annual option may be presented first for cash flow and commitment, with a monthly plan as a downsell.
- “Cold to sold” content: Create an evergreen video in a familiar format, such as a podcast. Lead with proof, explain the mechanism, and answer buying questions. Put it on the thank-you page and send it to booked prospects.
- Hotline as both delivery and content: Ask a member to state their niche, current and target monthly revenue, and obstacle. Coach the actual bottleneck live and edit the conversation into content; the goal and unresolved problem create tension.
- Funnel congruence: Make every step, from ad through call, match the same avatar, pain, desire, and mechanism. Audit the funnel as a cold visitor to find mismatches.
- Audience qualification pyramid: The pyramid's small top has ideal, high-retention clients; the broad base has more prospects but weaker fit. Set criteria by balancing quality and volume. Ads allow narrow targeting; organic traffic may require a broader pool that still meets the minimum capability threshold.
- Calls over optimization: Most members need more people to know about the offer. Prioritize calls through outreach and content; ads are advanced and carry platform and spending risk.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. If cohort gaps are a problem, test weekly enrollment: members complete the Backbone worksheet, join Monday backbone review, then Friday launch. Tuesday joiners wait until the next Monday. Launch unlocks after one day. The Friday call was planned to combine launch attract, convert, and deliver; after attract, members proceed at their own pace as calls and customers come in.
2. Put cohort over-delivers in the classroom for people joining between starts. Examples were a free hat, a recorded backbone-review invitation, and a video linking to sales scripts. Frame the gift's value and use a welcome video to show members greeting newcomers.
3. A course, community, and open Q&A may not provide enough accountability. Proactive check-ins, customer success, and cohort structure were presented as ways to improve implementation, not requirements for every business.
4. Examples were $1,950/month for four months, then $850/month, and another operator's $1,700/month for three months, then about $800 to $900. The initial phase carried more value, so the price could drop after two to four months. The team planned to pitch a paid-in-full year first, then installments. These are examples, not market rates.
5. Produce a “cold to sold” video using a podcast-style format with the core proof points, the mechanism, and the important buying triggers. Put it on the thank-you page after an application and send it to a booked prospect before the call. The purpose is to reduce time spent explaining basics during sales conversations and make the asset reusable beyond a single week's social reach.
6. Use a short hotline application, select members, clarify their constraint live, record and edit clips. For anonymity, change the voice and blur the face. The format was experimental and intended to serve members while generating content.
7. Track spend, applications, qualification, calls booked, close rate, cash collected, CAC, and ROAS. Separate collected cash from estimated LTV. Ads can spend quickly and require Meta Ads Manager skill, so beginners should focus elsewhere.
8. For a free group, build it for the paying-customer avatar, not for an unrelated beginner audience. Give the prospect the proof, backstory, pain-point content, and information needed to decide whether to book a call. A free group is only one possible container; a video or another funnel can carry the same information. If one-on-one coaching is sold through a freemium group, use the tier or plan page to show that a paid offer exists. At the time of recording, a prominent “upgrade” button was described as a future feature, not a currently released setting.
9. Review qualification criteria based on the source of leads. The example business's minimum cutoff was a valuable outcome and ability to help clients. Ads allowed tighter targeting for people with an audience or network; organic leads could be broader if still serviceable.
10. To change a member's payment tier, the team updated the plan and directed them to the group's `/plans` page. Verify current Skool behavior first.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- Hotline participant, opening prompt: “My Skool helps [who] to [result]. I make [amount] a month. I'd like to make [amount] a month, but what's stopping me is [obstacle].” Use for a concise live coaching submission. The speaker advised keeping the answer short enough to understand quickly.
- Example hotline framing: “I run a bookkeeping firm doing about $1.1 million a year… but I dread Monday mornings.” The clip began with the member's current position and tension, giving the viewer a reason to stay for the answer.
- Ad example, speaker: “Do you have over 10,000 followers and you're an educational creator?” Followed by proof and a direct invitation to apply for help monetizing an audience. Use the hook to qualify by audience size and role.
- Pre-call message, speaker's planned use: send the prospect one video “that when it comes to the call, you're ready to hit the ground running.” Use after a prospect books but before the call; no exact finalized message was provided.
- Member greeting video, speaker: “If you are an educational creator, you are here to scale your Skool to $10,000 to $100,000 a month. You are in the right place.” Use as an example opening for onboarding, then have the community say “Welcome to scaling” and direct the new member to the gift.
## Examples and case studies (member or company, what they did, the numbers and result; only what the transcript says)
- The team attributed growth from $0 to $300,000/month in 12 months to cohorts. A prior “100” season enrolled 88 clients in one month; the team planned large competitions a few times yearly while testing weekly starts.
- The team planned a three-month hybrid test. Matt Rice, reported at $30,000/month and experienced in backbone reviews, might gradually take more calls if member feedback stayed positive. Results were unknown.
- In the team's ad test, started July 7, spend rose from $200 per day to $1,100 per day, about $30,000 per month. Applications rose from seven on day one to about 30 to 40 per day. About 74% of applicants were qualified; 45% of applicants booked a call, for 82 calls total. Reported cost per booked call ranged from $42 to $50. The close rate was 12%, below the team's usual 40% to 50%, and it had produced 10 customers in the first 12 days. Cash collected was $19,500; acquisition cost was stated as $766, rounded to about $800, with 2.5x return on first-payment cash collected. At an estimated $9,000 lifetime value per customer, the speaker projected $90,000 from about $8,000 ad spend, or over 10x on LTV. The campaign and funnel were only about two weeks old; the LTV figure was a projection, not collected revenue.
- Cold-to-sold proof points included $0 to $300,000/month in 12 months, a competition average of $70,000 in 90 days, a 600,000-subscriber channel and $100,000 monthly revenue, and a client moving from $20,000 to $200,000/month. These were speaker claims, not verified here.
- For the hotline, the team adapted a format they had seen used elsewhere. The speaker emphasized that a real Skool owner naming current revenue, desired revenue, and an obstacle creates stakes, while the live answer demonstrates expertise. Editing outcomes were not yet available.
## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not treat the weekly hybrid model, ads, the upgrade button, or the hotline as established best practice. The speakers repeatedly called them new experiments and said results would follow.
- Do not copy a new Skool monetization model simply because the team is testing it. The team had an incentive to pioneer because it teaches Skool growth; most members were advised to follow proven steps and experiment in their own subject area.
- Do not create a free group for an avatar different from the one targeted by ads. Mismatched onboarding, course, and offer can stall conversion.
- Do not count estimated lifetime value as collected cash or assume a two-week ad funnel has an optimized close rate.
- Do not add complexity without a need. A public 15-minute backbone-review scheduler had been tested and abandoned because it was not accurate enough; the team returned to a standard queue.
- Do not start ads casually if platform complexity or spend could distract from the current call-booking bottleneck.
- Do not spend all working time on funnel tweaks while neglecting outreach, content output, and calls.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this call when a member is choosing between cohort and evergreen delivery, considering recurring high-ticket payment structures, wants a reusable content asset, or is deciding whether ads or a free group fit the current funnel. Ask what is already working, what the offer promises, who the paying avatar is, the member's current application-to-call and close rates, whether paid traffic can be supported, and which delivery problem exists now. Treat the experimental pricing and platform settings as historical examples, not default recommendations.
