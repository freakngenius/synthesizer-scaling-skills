# The Scientific Method To Scaling (Client Attraction Call)
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: not stated in source (1,824 transcript lines)
## One-paragraph summary
This client-attraction call teaches members to diagnose growth through a small set of tracked inputs and to focus on the current constraint, usually the number of sales calls booked. The central operating loop is measure activity, determine what correlates with calls, do more of the activities that work, and avoid interpreting small or noisy samples as proof of a conversion problem. Advice ranges from framing calls as valuable without disguising the sales purpose, simplifying qualification and follow-up, and using the right CTA for the user's stage, to improving setter workflow, lead tracking and community onboarding. The host repeatedly prioritizes call volume and clear data over premature optimization.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **Tracker as a constraint finder.** Track a short set of activity and sales metrics, compare them with calls booked, and identify which activities precede calls. Add a channel metric such as LinkedIn impressions or profile visits, then keep the one that correlates more strongly.
- **30-call rule.** High-ticket results are lumpy, so do not diagnose closing from a few calls. Reach 30 calls before deciding there is a close-rate problem. If close rate is above 20%, the host says to usually focus elsewhere.
- **Valuable sales call.** Frame the sales call around a useful outcome, such as diagnosing a constraint and creating a roadmap. Keep it a sales call; do not disguise a free service or teach the prospect to do the whole job on the call.
- **Pain and desire.** Create a reason to book by asking about the prospect's problem, frustration and time spent, then ask what result they want. If they are not a fit for the current offer, learn what they are interested in before assuming the offer cannot serve them.
- **Quality versus quantity.** More qualification and friction may improve call quality but reduce bookings. Tune the intake to the current constraint. If close rate is high, more calls may matter more than maintaining the same close rate.
- **Setter funnel.** Track conversations, links sent and calls booked. For individual leads, use three statuses: has potential, calendar link sent, booked. Compare conversion between setters and examine call framing when results differ.
- **Community and speed to lead.** A free group can nurture an audience over time if it collects contact details and someone follows up. Contact new members while they are active; use an onboarding call to identify goals and fit, then book a separate sales call when appropriate.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Fill in the tracker daily. The host estimates 30 seconds. Revenue is the full expected sale if an instalment plan completes; cash is what the buyer paid on day one. Do not count later instalments from prior months as new revenue.
2. Compare early-stage inputs with calls booked. For LinkedIn, track impressions and/or profile views rather than post count alone. One member described 1,000 to 20,000 impressions, with two-step engagement posts often producing 5,000 to 10,000 and sometimes about 20,000.
3. Wait for 30 calls before diagnosing close rate. Count an attended call as a customer, no deal, no offer, payment-plan issue, or refund. A true no-show is a no-show. If a call is rescheduled, update its date to when it occurred.
4. Pick the CTA for the stage. During a launch, make booking a call the primary action. Before a launch, a free community can collect contacts for later; if calls are unavailable, use a waitlist form for name and phone, then send useful resources and follow up.
5. In DMs, ask about pain and desired outcome, respond to the stated challenge with a relevant example, then explain how a call can help. Do not send a bare link without context. Follow up when someone has a link but has not booked: use a brief reminder for volume, or ask a promising lead whether they need a time or have questions.
6. Qualify without opening with “this is expensive.” Ask neutral questions about work or income. If needed, state the minimum payment plan and ask if it could fit for a perfect solution. If they say no, ask whether funds are unavailable or allocated elsewhere. Income is only an indicator, not a certain predictor; route a non-buyer to free material.
7. Track calendar links and bookings separately. One cited operation had 47 bookings from 77 links, about 60%. A high close rate plus lower link-to-book conversion may indicate that the booking page scares away otherwise interested people.
8. For a small Skool group, use a 30-to-60-second welcome video and a longer 10-to-20-minute classroom orientation. Ask members to introduce themselves. The host's target is 10 regulars; posts can help discovery and leaderboard participation.
9. With multiple setters, split lead pools, such as new group joins and existing members. Mark who owns each lead by a CRM tag or by liking a comment. The host mentions Scoot for Skool leads and Sendblue for calls/texts, but says a phone can suffice and Sendblue was still being tested.
10. For a simple outreach tally, use pen and paper or move a coin/sticker between containers per outreach. A benchmark given was about 100 outreaches and four booked calls daily. Review scripts if bookings lag.
11. Find groups that are both active and full of the target buyer. The host suggests spending two to three hours reviewing Skool groups, then Facebook or LinkedIn groups if needed. A large but inactive group is not automatically useful.
12. For setters, start with warmest leads: people already called who did not buy, then new members or people engaging with content. Keep a closer focused on calls and follow-up with their own pipeline rather than assigning them both closing and all new setting.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Host, booking-page promise:** “The goal of the call is to diagnose the number one thing holding you back from scaling and create a custom scaling roadmap to solve it in 30 days.” Use to make a sales call feel useful.
- **Setter, personalized invitation:** “Would it be helpful to get on a call with me or someone on my team, where we can show you how we've helped them and talk about how we could potentially help you too?” Use after acknowledging the person's pain and sharing a relevant example.
- **Setter, financial check:** “Is that something that's possibly in your range, if it was a perfect fit?” Use after stating the minimum payment option.
- **Setter, follow-up:** “Have you been able to find a time that works? Or do you still have any questions? Happy to answer them.” Use after sending a booking link.
- **Setter, clarify a no:** “Does that mean that the funds don't exist, or is it more that the money needs to be allocated somewhere else?” Use to distinguish lack of funds from a value or priority concern.
- **Host, free-content route:** “The best use of your time is to continue to consume my free content on Instagram.” Use when the minimum investment does not fit.
- **Host, onboarding call:** Offer to welcome a new community member and help them find their way around, then ask about their goals and what they are struggling with. Route a fit to a sales call; otherwise direct them to the free course.
## Examples and case studies (member or company, what they did, the numbers and result; only what the transcript says)
- **Vera, sewing offer:** January tracker showed 15,000 YouTube views, 1,500 outreach, 2,800 new followers, eight Skool posts, 300 new members and six calls kept. Only nine calls had been booked over the prior months, so the advice was to focus on call volume and frame the call as a 30-day sewing project plan.
- **Parisa, LinkedIn:** Two-step engagement posts generated about 5,000 to 10,000 impressions on average and sometimes nearly 20,000; one thread had close to 800 back-and-forth comments. They were published about twice weekly, so the experiment was to try daily posting.
- **Time Theory:** The host says the business reached $100,000 per month, sold at $3,800 standard and $1,900 for students, and cancelled 80% to 90% of booked calls in one experiment. High-ticket sales still made more than the low-ticket alternative.
- **Appointment setter:** A new full-time caller booked six or eight calls on the first day, according to the host's uncertain recollection. Another setter benchmark was about four calls per day from 100 outreaches.
- **Group selection:** Chris posted widely for landowners. The host recommended prioritizing active groups of landowners already interested in monetizing land, not simply groups with large membership counts.
## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not infer a close-rate problem from a small sample; first reach 30 calls.
- Do not use non-sales calls to get attendance. Keep the purpose honest and the promised outcome useful.
- Do not reject prospects based only on age, income or assumptions about affordability. Qualify them; the host cites students and low-income buyers who purchased.
- Do not count a sent calendar link as a booked call. Follow up and track the conversion.
- Do not equate post count with reach; track impressions or profile visits when they better explain calls.
- Do not build a complicated CRM for every interaction. Track potential, link sent and booked, and mark lead ownership.
- Do not make cold outreach more aggressive after a platform warning. The host recommends content and networking instead.
- Do not choose a community by size alone; check activity and buyer fit.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this call's methods when a member has some lead activity but cannot tell whether the bottleneck is reach, booking, attendance or closing; when a calendar link gets accepted but not booked; when a CRM is overbuilt; or when the member is deciding between content-to-call and community nurturing. Before advising, ask for the current stage, monthly or weekly tracker data, number of calls booked and taken, link-to-book count, channel and audience, current CTA, qualification questions, and whether the member is launching now or building an audience before launch. Ask how many calls are in the sample before making a close-rate diagnosis.
