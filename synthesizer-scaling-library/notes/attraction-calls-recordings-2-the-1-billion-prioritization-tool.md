# The $1 Billion Prioritization Tool (Client Attraction Call)
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: not stated in source (2,205 transcript lines)
## One-paragraph summary
This call introduces a simple roadmap spreadsheet for choosing work instead of reacting to every new idea. Its core rule is to place all possible projects in a backlog, select the highest-priority work, keep the active list very short, finish work, and record what shipped. The roadmap is paired with an attraction review: inspect actual traffic sources, identify which platform and activities produce the right people, then invest more in what is working. The call also teaches practical competitor-content research, offer and audience fit, community-page testing, and how traffic volume should influence the complexity of the conversion path. The title's “$1 billion” refers to the scale of Skool and the founder's use of a roadmap, not a promise that the spreadsheet alone creates that result.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **Roadmap spreadsheet.** Four main tabs: Backlog, Up Next, Work in Progress and Shipped, plus an optional Program Improvement tab. Backlog parks unranked ideas; Up Next holds prioritized future work; Work in Progress is the active project; Shipped records completion. The flow is Backlog to Up Next to Work in Progress to Shipped.
- **Strategy as allocation.** Strategy is deciding what to do, when and in what order with limited time. Keep active projects to one if possible, maximum three for a small team. Capture new ideas instead of switching tasks whenever one appears.
- **Traffic-source diagnosis.** Skool's Traffic Sources view reflects last touch before joining, not always the first discovery source. Compare platform reporting with member answers, especially when direct URLs, email, link shorteners or discovery search may affect attribution.
- **Attraction system.** Long term, publish useful content where the target audience spends attention and become a trusted guide for a specific problem. Short term, active Skool or Facebook group networking can create calls sooner. Revisit network contacts and follow up monthly.
- **Competitor research.** Find relevant channels, sort content by most popular, collect titles and visual elements, then look for recurring patterns. Where the niche has few examples, borrow a format from adjacent niches and adapt it to the actual audience.
- **Content-performance clues.** The call names popular figures, recency, relevance, curiosity, specific language/numbers, unusualness, simplicity and conflict as possible contributors to performance. They are clues, not guarantees, and should serve the offer's audience.
- **Proof, Promise, Plan.** Establish why the creator has credibility, state the viewer's benefit, and preview the steps. The speaker derived this from reviewing best-performing introductions repeatedly.
- **Match conversion mechanism to traffic.** Low traffic calls for more direct outreach to make use of each lead. More traffic can support a scalable content-to-VSL-to-call path. When the source and audience are clearly working, deepen effort there before adding a new platform.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. State the business goal. Put all potential projects in Backlog, including good ideas that are not timely.
2. When current work is complete, review Up Next and Backlog. Move the single project most likely to advance the goal into Work in Progress. Mark it Shipped only when done. Keep active work to one project where possible, maximum three for a small operation.
3. Keep curriculum improvements separate if needed. Record missing examples, sticking points and small improvements in Program Improvement so they do not displace the main growth priority.
4. Open Skool Settings and review Traffic Sources. Screenshot the data and pair it with a question about channel priority. When member polls conflict with platform reporting, export members as CSV, analyze their answers in Sheets or Excel, and compare. Platform data may show the last action that caused joining.
5. Research content on YouTube by searching the niche, finding relevant channels, sorting videos by “Most popular,” and copying titles and thumbnail elements into a sheet. In one Skool niche, the researcher excluded videos below 10,000 views; adjust the cutoff to the size and activity of the niche.
6. Tag recurring themes or formats and count how often they appear among strong examples. Study adjacent niches if current niche examples are sparse; adapt a proven format, not another creator's unsupported claims.
7. Structure the introduction with Proof, Promise, Plan. Use evidence the creator can substantiate, a clear viewer outcome, and a specific process.
8. If a free group's About-page conversion is low, test a simple headline and one clear lead magnet/outcome instead of requiring a visitor to watch a video. Record a baseline, make one large change, then compare after seven days. Review mobile presentation and visitor fit as well as desktop design.
9. Choose content versus outreach based on traffic. With little attention, do more direct outreach. If a channel produces enough relevant viewers or community joins, focus on content and use a VSL or call-booking path. If both work, combine content with a setter contacting new members.
10. For a YouTube sprint, try one video per day for 30 days. The speaker mentions 22 completed videos, at least 25 videos, and a rule about not missing more than one day; the wording is ambiguous, so treat it as a consistency challenge rather than a hard rule.
11. Use Facebook groups when the audience is not on Skool: offer useful public help, create content for members, build relationships with owners/admins, and become a trusted participant. Use YouTube or Instagram for longer-term audience building where those are the audience's platforms.
12. Define who the offer is and is not for, and make titles speak to the desired buyer. If content says “If you're broke, watch this,” it will attract people who identify as broke.
13. Ask permission before using a client's name or face in public material when the relationship or consent is unclear.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not let every new idea interrupt active work. Capture it in Backlog.
- Do not keep more than one to three projects active in a small team.
- Do not mistake last-touch attribution for first discovery; compare with member responses.
- Do not assume a low page conversion is only a design problem. Traffic may not match the intended audience.
- Do not assume a video improves conversion; test against a baseline, including mobile display.
- Do not assume a platform is proven from one sale or one week of data. Keep testing until the pattern is clear.
- Do not add platforms by default. One source can support substantial growth when it reaches the right audience.
- Do not use a member's face or name publicly without considering permission.
- Do not promise that a daily-video challenge guarantees growth; the call provides no evidence that supports a guarantee.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use the roadmap when a person is distracted by too many projects, cannot choose a priority, or is about to add a channel, tool or course. Use the attraction sections when someone has traffic but does not know its source, cannot identify which channel to prioritize, or is creating content without studying what performs. Before applying it, ask for the person's primary goal, current bottleneck, projects in progress, team capacity, traffic sources, audience and offer, and available evidence such as views, calls and sales. Ask whether the next priority is short-term call generation or long-term audience building. For page optimization, establish the current conversion rate and where the visitors come from before changing the page.
