# Discovery Training
Type: lesson   Stage: Launch Convert   Length/size: 45:19 video transcript plus training notes
## One-paragraph summary
This lesson teaches the first half of a sales call: prepare, frame the conversation, diagnose the prospect's current and desired situations, and transition into an offer presentation. Its main model is the Gap Framework. A prospect buys when they see a gap between their current position and a desired result, feel the cost of staying where they are, and believe the seller can provide a bridge. The lesson gives five discovery checkpoints and sample questions, while emphasizing natural dialogue rather than mechanically reading a script. It frames discovery as diagnosis, with the prospect's answers supplying the evidence for a tailored pitch and early identification of objections.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- Sales call process: pre-call prep, intro and framing, discovery, transition, pitch, commitment, investment, then objections and close. This lesson focuses on the first four stages.
- Gap Framework: establish where the prospect is now, where they want to be, and the bridge between those points. The seller first identifies the gap, then later presents the offer as a bridge across it.
- Five discovery checkpoints:
  1. Primary motivation: why they booked and what first attracted them.
  2. Current situation: what they do now, including work, offer, price, methods, and systems relevant to the niche.
  3. Pain and problems: biggest challenge, causes, failed attempts, duration, and effects.
  4. Desired situation: the 12-month goal, reasons it matters, and practical and emotional consequences of achieving it.
  5. Cost of inaction: what happens if the situation remains unchanged, including time, energy, money, and lost progress.
- Three buying beliefs named in the lesson: the prospect wants to cross the gap faster; does not know how to cross it alone; or wants a clear roadmap and guide. The seller should establish which belief fits rather than assume that everyone needs the same help.
- Transition: invite the prospect to disclose anything else that may affect the decision, address early concerns, state evidence-based confidence in the fit, and ask permission to present.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. About five minutes before the call, tidy the background, present yourself professionally, choose a quiet room, use headphones, print the script and have a pen, review the prospect's application and social profiles, and review earlier messages. Record the call so the seller can improve the pitch and learn from the prospect's language.
2. Do six physiological sighs: take a deep breath, hold, add a sharp additional inhale through the nose, then exhale slowly through the mouth. The instructor presents this as a way to settle attention before a call, especially when calls are back to back.
3. Join on time. The suggested call is 45 minutes, with a 30-60-minute general range. Intro and framing take about 1-2 minutes; discovery takes about 20-30 minutes; the transition takes about 1 minute.
4. Begin with light rapport. Then ask whether they have something to take notes on and frame the call: you will ask about their situation and goal; if there is a fit, explain the offer; they can decide whether to join. This sets an agenda and gives the prospect control.
5. Ask why they booked. If they came through DMs, say you have already chatted there so the question does not sound as if you forgot the context. If they answer vaguely, ask what they mean or invite more detail. A brief affirmation of effort can reinforce an action-taking identity before moving into the current situation.
6. Customize current-situation questions to the user's niche. Examples include what they sell and how they price it, how they currently attract customers, what system they use, or what their current health, fitness, or creative practice looks like.
7. Explore pain through open follow-ups: what is the biggest challenge, why has it not been solved, what have they tried, and how long has it been going on? Reflect what you heard and ask how repeated attempts have felt. The goal is to understand the problem, not to impose a diagnosis.
8. Move to the desired situation. Ask for a 12-month goal, why it matters, and what would change in the person's life. Ask for tangible specifics and emotional effects. The instructor recommends progressing gradually from easy questions to deeper ones.
9. Ask about the cost of inaction: the impact of the current struggle, what happens if nothing changes over the next six months, whether that future is something they want to avoid, and what the problem costs in time, energy, or money. Use a curious, gentle tone. A retrospective question asks whether a roadmap six months earlier would have left them closer to the goal now.
10. Double-check commitment with a 1-10 question. The prospect should explain their own commitment; do not substitute your confidence for their answer.
11. Before pitching, ask whether there is anything else you should know. If they say no, the transcript recommends asking once more. If a concern emerges, clarify and address it before the pitch. Give specific reasons you believe you can help, based on their situation and relevant proof, then ask permission to explain the offer.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- Coach, framing: “I’ll ask you a couple of questions around where you’re at, what you’re working on, and where you want to go. Then if it sounds like we’re a good fit, I can walk you through what it looks like to work together.” Use after rapport and note-taking check.
- Coach, motivation: “What got you interested in booking this call in the first place?” If booked via DMs, preface it by acknowledging the prior conversation.
- Coach, clarify: “When you say ___, what do you mean?” or “Could you explain that in a little more depth?” Use when an answer is general or unclear.
- Coach, current situation: “What are you doing for work right now?” and “What do you sell? How do you price it?” Add niche-specific questions about present methods and systems.
- Coach, diagnose pain: “What do you think is the biggest challenge right now?” “Why do you think you haven’t solved it yet?” “What have you tried already?” “How long has this been going on?”
- Coach, goal: “What is your 12 month goal for the [thing]?” Follow with “What’s your motivation for getting to [goal]?” and “How would getting to [goal] impact other areas of your life?”
- Coach, inaction: “What happens if nothing changes over the next 6 months?” Use a supportive, curious tone, then ask about the cost in time, energy, or money.
- Coach, transition: “Before we potentially move on, what else should I know about you or your situation?” Use to invite concerns before pitching.
- Coach, fit and permission: “Based on everything you’ve told me I 100% believe we can help you because [reasons based on their situation and your proof]. Would you like me to walk you through what we do and how it works?” Replace the placeholder with verified evidence.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not use a rigid sequence of complex, programmed questions. The script is a set of checkpoints that guides a natural conversation.
- Do not avoid pain questions merely because they feel awkward, but do not turn the call into an interrogation. The lesson advocates an inquisitive, gentle tone and says not to take pain agitation to an extreme.
- Do not read the script word for word or treat showing up as entitlement to a sale. Read it, visualize the conversation, rehearse, role-play, and take calls until the checkpoints feel natural.
- Do not focus on closing before you understand the gap. Accurate diagnosis is the purpose of this part of the call.
- The instructor uses forceful metaphors about intensifying pain, including making a prospect cry. This is the instructor's framing, not a requirement to pressure someone or invent distress; the same lesson also calls for authentic dialogue and a supportive tone.

- Pre-call prep: about 5 minutes.
- Intro and framing: about 1-2 minutes.
- Discovery: about 20-30 minutes.
- Transition: about 1 minute.
- Sales call: 45 minutes suggested, with 30-60 minutes given as the average range.
- Six physiological sighs are recommended before the call.
- Discovery asks about a 12-month desired goal and a six-month cost-of-inaction horizon.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use when someone has booked a sales call and needs to prepare, diagnose fit, or practice moving from discovery to pitch. Ask what the offer helps with, what the prospect has already said, what evidence supports the seller's ability to help, whether a partner is part of the decision, and how long the call is. Ask the user to identify their niche-specific current-situation questions and to avoid inserting outcomes or proof they cannot substantiate.
## Open questions or things the source left unclear
The lesson does not define an ethical boundary or stopping rule for probing personal pain, despite its forceful language about agitation. It does not provide a rubric for deciding when discovery is complete, nor an exact script for every niche. It gives no pricing guidance, conversion benchmarks, or verified outcomes for the hypothetical examples. The three buying beliefs are presented as core reasons prospects buy, but the lesson does not say how to diagnose which one applies when several do.
