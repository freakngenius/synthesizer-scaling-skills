# Make This Video, It's Blowing Up Skools
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: 2,765 transcript lines, including a 16:48 intro-video transcript
## One-paragraph summary
The call teaches an evergreen credibility video that compiles a creator's proof without turning into a brag reel. Build it from career seasons, and for each season connect a problem the audience recognizes, a story of how the creator worked through it, the lesson learned, and evidence of an outcome. Use the finished video as a pinned channel introduction and repurpose it across the community and other content channels. The host also explains how to choose and test proof, how to record from an outline, and when this asset belongs in the sequence: after the rapid launch and outreach to existing warm contacts.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **Proof-first niche positioning:** The larger the niche, the stronger the proof needs to be. If the creator cannot claim the strongest proof in a broad market, narrow the market until the proof stands out. The speaker uses Synthesizer Scaling's results in Skool as a narrower competitive space than business generally. After identifying strong proof, reuse it throughout the Backbone, channel, community, and sales assets.
- **Credibility intro video:** Make a single video that tells the creator's career story through selected seasons, combining proof and useful teaching. It should help viewers understand what the creator has done and why they can help. A video can remain pinned for years while its claims stay current, and can also appear in end screens, Skool posts, or as the first classroom video.
- **Proof, story, lesson:** The basic ingredients of each season. Proof establishes a result; the story gives context; the lesson makes the experience useful to the audience. The talk also describes the same sequence as a resonant problem, an epiphany or change, and a proof win, followed by a broadly useful lesson. The story lets the audience recognize a problem they have, see that the creator once faced it, and learn a route to an outcome they want.
- **Epiphany bridge:** A customer or creator story that goes from “I used to struggle with the same problem” to a turning point, then to an outcome. The lesson drawn should be clear enough to apply elsewhere. Testimonials can use the same structure.
- **Brainstorm then order:** First list possible career seasons, proof, stories, and lessons without trying to make the list polished. Then select the strongest examples and arrange them into a sequence. Work from a lesson to the story and proof, or start from proof or an interesting season and find the lesson.
- **Hook structure:** The sample hook uses proof, plan, and callout, then moves into the first lesson. Proof is a concise stack of credentials and outcomes; plan previews the sections and what the viewer will learn; callout names the audience and their problem. The attached template includes a “pain” field as well. The example recording states the callout after proof and plan and begins the first lesson immediately afterward.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. **Choose the title.** Use a specific period of experience, niche, and outcome. The example was “I Spent 5 Years of Scaling Skools to $100,000+/month.” Caleb Ralston's example was “16 years of building brands to 30 million plus followers.” Include the years, the field, and the result.
2. **Name two audiences.** Write the name of a real person who is a strong fit for the offer and already knows the creator somewhat but does not know the full proof. The primary audience is the dream customer. The secondary audience is a broader person who can still be helped.
3. **Brainstorm proof and seasons.** List important phases and results, including personal results and client outcomes. Examples from the speaker's brainstorm included the first Skool investment, client growth, channel growth, being broke or confused, and hosting Skool Games. Do not polish the first draft.
4. **Pair the season with its audience relevance.** Ask what problem the audience has, what changed for the creator, what proof resulted, and what general lesson can help the audience. Prefer a specific problem the audience recognizes. Use client evidence and personal proof where appropriate.
5. **Select and order the strongest sections.** Keep the connection between the sections clear. The speaker said the full video showed six career seasons and ran about 15 to 16 minutes. A short post can use one season: hook, problem, epiphany, and proof.
6. **Make proof concrete and believable.** Use specific outcomes, numbers, timeframes, screenshots, dashboards, channel examples, or short client testimonial clips when permission and assets allow. Showing a skill can be stronger than merely stating it. Numbers are not mandatory, but specific, verifiable proof and a timeframe often strengthen a claim.
7. **Record from an outline.** The speaker filled out the template, checked the structure, and riffed on the key points rather than reading the script verbatim. He read a section, looked up, told the story, and moved on. The edited video was 16 minutes from a 28-minute recording. Multiple takes and editing are normal; a teleprompter is optional and the speaker cautioned that scripting and revisions for a teleprompter can take substantial time.
8. **Place and route the video.** Pin it on YouTube or the main channel where prospects discover the creator. Make it the channel homepage video if that fits, and link to it from end screens of videos already getting views. Add direct calls to action to book a call near the beginning and end if the goal is calls. Post or pin it in Skool and use it in onboarding or classroom where relevant.
9. **Test framing.** List different ways to state the proof, then test titles and framing through content, audience polls, community posts, or reactions on sales calls. Rank proof by strength if unsure. Use an existing thumbnail that has performed consistently or test alternatives with YouTube's thumbnail tester.
10. **Sequence the work.** Finish the rapid launch first: use existing network, audience, engaged followers, past customers, and previous call-bookers. Build new audience assets, including this video, after working through those existing relationships.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Creator, title:** “I spent [number] years [field or niche] to [specific outcome].” Use for the video title; the number of years, niche, and outcome are the key parts.
- **Creator, hook plan:** “In this video I'm going to break down the six seasons of my journey, the lessons I learned, the mistakes I made, and the things I'd do differently.” Use after the proof stack to set expectations.
- **Creator, audience callout:** “If you're an educational creator trying to build a Skool but feeling overwhelmed, unclear on your offer, or needing more traffic, this is for you.” Adapt the audience and problem to the creator's niche.
- **Creator, audience application of the One Source Rule:** “Learn from people ahead of you. When you find somebody who has achieved what you're trying to achieve, commit to implementing what they teach for the next 90 days before you consume anything else.” Use as a lesson within a story about information overload.
- **Creator, testimonial request:** Ask the client whether the proposed description of their result is fair and accurate before using it. The member's example was to describe the result, ask if the client was comfortable with the claim, and proceed only after agreement.
- **Creator, NDA proof:** “I can't share the name or the result because of an NDA.” Use only when confidentiality restricts what can be disclosed; the speaker said anonymized proof is less persuasive but better than no evidence.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not stack credentials with no audience value. A long brag reel can repel viewers. Pair proof with the story and a useful lesson.
- Do not overclaim client results or take credit for outcomes that were not caused by the creator's work. Ask the client to confirm the wording. Overstating a relationship can damage reputation.
- Do not let an NDA be a reason to fabricate or disclose protected information. Explain the limit and use only approved, supportable proof.
- Do not spend time making this video before completing rapid-launch actions that use existing relationships.
- Do not assume every season must have a perfect proof-problem-lesson pattern. The host included stories and lessons that mattered to the brand even when the proof or audience resonance was less direct.
- Avoid over-scripting if it causes unnatural delivery or too much revision. The host recommends an outline and practiced riffing rather than a polished teleprompter script.
- Do not judge proof only by your own doubts. Test how the audience responds.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this for an educator, coach, or consultant who has meaningful personal or client proof but has not made it clear in one concise, audience-serving asset. Before applying it, ask what offer and niche the creator is targeting, who the primary real-person viewer is, what results they can substantiate and disclose, which stories have resonated already, and whether rapid launch and warm-network outreach are complete. Also ask which platform can pin or prominently feature the video and whether the goal is awareness, booked calls, or both.
