# Build as You Go Training
Type: lesson   Stage: Launch Deliver   Length/size: 24:57 video transcript plus program outline example
## One-paragraph summary
Build a program through just-in-time delivery rather than preparing every lesson in advance. First create the phase outline and an onboarding welcome video, then build only the next delivery week before it begins. Start each phase by deciding what clients must do, then derive the modules and training that support those actions. Deliver the training, observe questions and client progress, and use that feedback to make the next week's material. Keep deleting content that does not support progress, and spend more time working with clients and improving from evidence than polishing materials in advance.

## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)

### Program hierarchy
A program consists of ordered **phases** (milestones toward the outcome), **modules** (knowledge needed to take action within a phase), and **actions** (what participants physically do). The outline provides the overall destination sequence, while modules and actions are filled in progressively.

### Just-in-time delivery
The cycle is: build onboarding right away; once clients buy and a start date is set, build Week 1 before the start; while participants complete Week 1, build Week 2; during Week 2, build Week 3; repeat. This allows the provider to respond to what participants liked, where they struggled, and what they need next. It also creates a deadline that limits perfectionism and keeps the provider close to the learner's experience. Build far enough ahead to deliver the next week, not the whole program in advance.

### Action-first reverse engineering
Although the hierarchy is described as phases, modules, then actions, the practical design process begins with actions. For the next phase, list the 3 to 5 steps a participant must complete to progress. Then ask what knowledge or instruction is needed for each action. Those needs become modules. Starting with modules can lead to too much training and too many action items; action-first design narrows the curriculum.

### Minimum viable training and iteration
For each action or module, create the smallest useful explanation, then record a walkthrough and publish it. The Synthesizer method uses Google Docs with a module title, training organized under headings, and action steps at the end. The provider presents the week's phase on a live call, releases prerecorded training, and gives specific action items. After someone goes through it, improve the material based on questions and outcomes. Delete material that does not support the next step.

### Practice what you teach, and innovate on delivery
The Synthesizer team recommends copying its process and formats, while adapting the content to the creator's expertise. The business-growth roadmap is presented as proven; the provider should innovate on how they deliver their own expertise and help their audience. A program is improved by bringing clients through it, seeing what works or fails, and revising from their feedback.

## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Finish the program phase outline and create the onboarding welcome video first. The phases tell clients what journey they are entering; the welcome video prepares them before delivery.
2. When a start date is set, prepare the first week before that date. Do not create all future weeks just in case.
3. Return to the program outline and choose the first phase to deliver. Break it into 3 to 5 actions that participants must take to advance.
4. For every action, ask what the participant needs to know or see to complete it. Make that content a module. Remove proposed actions and modules that are not needed for the immediate progress.
5. Organize the module in a Google Doc: give it a title, use headings for the training, and put the action steps at the end. The lesson says a video walkthrough of the training is sufficient; the material need not be visually elaborate.
6. Present the week's phase live, release prerecorded training videos for its modules, and provide clear action items. The instructor describes a weekly live kickoff call plus recorded training and actions.
7. As participants work through the current week, note their questions, obstacles, and what they need. Use these observations to build the next week's module and include recurring questions in the training or FAQ section.
8. Repeat week over week. Improve modules after people use them rather than attempting to predict every future need.
9. Keep operating the business while improving delivery. Work directly with clients, attract and convert more clients, and use more client experiences to discover what to keep, remove, or fix.
10. Use weekly implementation calls and community posts to ask questions and get feedback; share wins and copy the format of the Synthesizer Google Docs as a starting structure.

The Synthesizer example follows the phases Business Backbone, Rapid Launch, Attraction Systems, Conversion Systems, Delivery Systems, and Bonus Trainings. In the Rapid Launch phase, actions include a network and audience audit, research-call outreach and calls, launch posts and DMs, discovery and pitch, objection handling, and delivery setup. The training organizes these actions into Launch Prep, Launch Attract, Launch Convert, and Launch Deliver modules. Attraction Systems is deliberately left undeveloped until participants reach that phase.

## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Program creator, while designing the next phase:** "What action steps does someone have to take in order to progress?" Use this before writing training content.
- **Program creator, deriving a module:** "What do they need to do?" Then ask what they need to know to take that action. Use the answer to decide the module, not to expand the program with extra topics.
- **Program creator, planning the next delivery week:** Identify what participants asked during the current week and add recurring answers as short FAQ guidance or training notes in the next week's materials.
- **Google Doc module template:** Title with the module name; add training with headings; place the action steps at the end. Record a walkthrough of the document and repeat for later modules.
- **Client-facing weekly delivery sequence:** Present the phase on a live call, share prerecorded training for the modules, then assign specific actions. The source provides this as a process, not a verbatim script.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not build the full program just in case someone might need it. Build the next week just in time.
- Do not start by listing every training topic. Start with participant actions and reverse engineer the needed modules.
- Do not add unnecessary material or spend most of the time designing polished Notion pages, Canva slides, lead magnets, or elaborate training assets instead of working with clients.
- Do not assume that a template can supply the creator's expertise. Copy the process and formats, then adapt the substance to the audience and the creator's experience.
- Do not wait for a perfect program before beginning. The outline and materials are expected to develop as clients progress.
- Do not stop attracting and converting clients in order to focus only on delivery. Client volume and feedback help reveal what needs to improve.
- Do not develop later phases prematurely. Leave attraction, conversion, delivery, or bonus modules for the point when clients are ready for them.
- Do not interpret just-in-time as poor preparation. Week 1 is built before the start date; then each following week is prepared during the preceding week.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this when a creator has sold or is preparing to deliver an educational or coaching offer but feels pressure to build a complete course before working with clients. Ask when the first cohort or client begins, what phase they need to complete first, what concrete actions advance them, what knowledge is missing for those actions, and how the creator will hear client questions during delivery. Check that onboarding and a phase outline exist before applying the weekly build cycle. The approach is especially relevant when the creator has real expertise but does not yet know which teaching materials clients need.
