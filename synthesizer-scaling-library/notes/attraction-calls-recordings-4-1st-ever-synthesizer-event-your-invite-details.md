## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **Prepare for the worst, hope for the best:** Set an outcome, then ask what volume or action would make failure unreasonable. Plan for obstacles rather than assuming each step will go well. Expect noes and slow feedback, but continue executing the actions. The example answer is often more volume, such as sustained outreach or daily content, not a clever shortcut.
- **“Does this book me more calls?” test:** Before taking on a task, identify how it can produce booked calls. If there is no clear connection, defer or drop it. Website details, funnels, plugins, affiliate setup, tiers, and classroom work are often lower priority than increasing traffic and calls. This is a prioritization filter, not a claim that those assets never matter.
- **Three levels of business relationships:** People ahead of you can mentor; people behind you can become clients; peers at a similar stage can share lessons, support and build alongside you. The peer group is often neglected, and in-person events can strengthen those ties.
- **Competition design:** Define the purpose first, choose a clear, verifiable metric that reflects that purpose, and set incentives that move participants toward it. In a free group, the objective may be activation and booked sales calls; in a paid group, better client results. For fair comparison, measure new cash flow rather than total revenue when members begin at very different starting points. Prizes can reward access and status, not only cash.
- **Customer results as the decision anchor:** When a business decision trades off customer outcomes against short-term margin or convenience, the presenters prioritize customer results. Their reasoning is that strong results support referrals, retention, proof and future growth. The aim is to become excellent at helping a defined niche reach its desired outcome, then become known for that work.
- **Macro versus micro focus:** Micro focus means avoiding momentary distractions; macro focus means not starting extra businesses, offers, products or channels. The speakers treat macro focus as the larger risk. Keep returning to the single highest-priority item on the roadmap.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Write down the goal before choosing activities. Ask what actions and repetition level would make missing that goal unreasonable. Assume the path will include rejection and delayed feedback.
2. Review the day's planned tasks using the booked-call test. Give priority to actions tied to attracting qualified people, starting conversations and booking calls. Avoid adopting a new funnel or project just because it is novel.
3. Repeat actions that have already worked. Keep the core program roadmap stable, but test new attraction angles, content ideas and campaign framing. A new campaign can re-present a proven offer without changing what the program teaches.
4. For a community challenge, select a measure members can verify. The call's “100” tracked new cash flow over 90 days. New cash flow was chosen so a member already earning a large amount would not win solely on their starting revenue.
5. When planning a goal with a team, translate the revenue target into capacity constraints. The call uses a hypothetical $300,000/month business needing 100 calls/month; if the owner cannot take that many calls, consider a sales representative, then appointment-setting capacity, then coaching or customer-success capacity as delivery requires. These are illustrative dependencies, not a universal staffing plan.
6. If distracted, reduce the scope of the day. List possible tasks, identify the one core action, and cross off or delete the rest. One speaker uses a fixed bedtime, 9 p.m. in the example, to stop work and protect the following day's capacity.
7. For persistence, revisit community “win” posts when motivation drops. The speaker filters posts by “Top” and “Year” and reads member progress stories to remember that others have moved through slow starts.
8. For a competition, consider prizes with a repeatable value: direct access to the coach, recognition or status, certificates, podiums, and an in-person experience. The most suitable prize depends on the challenge's purpose and the program's capacity.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not expect a handful of outreach messages or calls to produce an immediate client. Persist through no replies, no-shows and lost sales.
- Do not let poor expectations make you quit when the process takes longer than hoped.
- Do not spend substantial time on a task when you cannot explain how it books more calls or advances the current roadmap.
- Do not keep adding offers, channels or projects. The speakers warn that new projects can consume time without producing progress.
- Do not change the core program system without a reason. Innovate in attraction angles and messaging while following the established delivery and launch path.
- Do not use an unverified contest metric or design a challenge around a goal that does not match its purpose.
- For an event restricted to members, do not share invitation details publicly or bring nonmembers. This is event-specific guidance, not a general marketing rule.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this call when a user is distracted by setup work, creating too many offers, losing momentum after weak responses, designing a member challenge, or deciding how to increase acquisition while preserving a proven delivery system. Ask what their near-term business goal is, which actions already book calls, what evidence they have about those actions, and what tasks are consuming time without a clear link to the goal. For a competition, ask whether its purpose is member activation, sales calls or paid-client outcomes, and what metric can be verified fairly. For capacity questions, ask about current call volume, close rate, service limits and the founder's desired work pattern before reusing the staffing illustration.
