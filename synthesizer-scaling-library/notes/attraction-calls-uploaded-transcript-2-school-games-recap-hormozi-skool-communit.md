# Client attraction call on entry requirements and fit
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: 1h23m
## One-paragraph summary
This call focuses on choosing whom an offer is for and how to attract those people through content, testimonials, and community messaging. The presenters recommend a low enough entry requirement to preserve a viable audience, with “having achieved a valuable outcome” as a filter when a group program needs participants who can benefit from a shared transformation. They also explain how to communicate the desired result without leading with a method prospects may resist, why one-to-one businesses can begin broadly and narrow over time, and how to make a cohort deliverable after individualized work becomes exhausting. The recurring principle is to execute the current model and solve the actual bottleneck instead of building extra tiers or chasing new tactics.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- Entry requirements: Start with a low barrier that still allows you to help customers get results. Raise it gradually as you learn which clients are difficult to serve or do not get results. Avoid jumping from “anyone” to a requirement so high that most of the available audience is excluded. For a cohort or course, participants need enough similarity to move through the same transformation; one-to-one work can accommodate more variation.
- Valuable outcome filter: For a program about growing a coaching, consulting, or community business, a prospect should have achieved a valuable outcome for themselves or others. This supports credibility and makes it more feasible to help them scale. The presenters say this filter leaves a large share of Synthesizer members eligible without restricting the market as much as a specific revenue or subscriber threshold.
- Attraction is shaped by what the creator says and shows: Content language, examples, and testimonials signal who the offer is for. Naming broke audiences or using testimonials only from beginners attracts more people at that level. Select examples to speak to different segments rather than letting every public example imply the same entry point.
- Sell the wanted outcome, then explain the method: Do not lead with “high ticket” if that label turns the audience away. Market the result and the problem solved; discuss the delivery or business model later, when it can be connected to the prospect's situation. The program's promise is to clarify the backbone, develop an offer the client wants to sell and deliver, and launch it. High ticket may be the right answer, but is not the entire identity of the offer.
- Saturate before expanding: A narrowly defined niche may have more potential customers than the coach assumes. Serve the current niche as fully as possible. If and when it is exhausted, expand to the closest adjacent niche or go down-market by helping people enter the niche, rather than starting a wholly new business.
- Cohort transition: Individualized delivery can be expensive in time. Preserve useful personalized feedback by doing it in a group session, or open with a one-to-one audit and move the rest of the program into group delivery. Reusable training and materials make later cohorts easier to run.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Write a clear answer to “Who don't I work with?” from the Backbone worksheet. Use the answer to guide qualification and how you describe the offer publicly.
2. Check the size of the audience implied by each requirement before adding a revenue, subscriber, or experience threshold. Start with a lower qualification line if the offer can still produce results; observe who buys, who succeeds, and who is hard to serve, then refine.
3. For group delivery, define the common transformation members need to complete. Use a qualification based on evidence of prior achievement where the program requires that foundation. For one-to-one services, sell more broadly at first and narrow based on delivery experience.
4. Audit public content and testimonials. Replace examples that unintentionally call in an audience you do not want with examples of the audience and outcomes you intend to serve. Use a deliberate mix of outcomes if the program serves people at more than one stage.
5. When prospects resist a method, return to their stated problem and desired outcome. Explain that the work will find the best offer or business model for their case; do not promise a specific method before examining the situation. Bring up premium or high-ticket delivery during a sales conversation if it fits, rather than making it an early marketing filter.
6. If a narrow niche seems small, work it thoroughly before expanding. The presenters suggest considering a close adjacent niche with only a small positioning change, or helping people enter the established niche, once the original group is genuinely well served.
7. When individual service becomes draining, identify the repeated parts. Deliver shared instruction and implementation support to a cohort, and consider one kickoff audit for individual needs. In the Scaling example, 60 members' individual Backbone feedback took about one to two full days; remaining delivery included a weekly call, community management, and DMs.
8. Use a free cohort or challenge to engage a free community when appropriate. The presenters say this produced stronger engagement than introducing low-ticket tiers in their example. Prioritize executing the existing model before testing new price or product structures.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Creator, content examples:** “Let's say you're making $10,000 and you want $20,000,” or “Let's say your business makes $30,000 and you want to make $50,000.” Use examples that attract the audience you want, rather than repeatedly addressing people who are broke.
- **Coach, prospect who dislikes high-ticket work:** “We'll get you clarity on what the best offer is for you.” Use when the prospect's desired result is offer clarity, without promising a particular price model before the Backbone work.
- **Coach, qualification conversation:** “Can I help this person?” If no, explain that the current program is not the right fit, suggest a useful next step such as a free roadmap or community, and offer to revisit later. Do not keep coaching for an hour when there is no fit.
- **Creator, niche review:** “Who don't I work with?” Use to define a practical boundary, then compare it with the audience the content actually attracts.
## Examples and case studies (member or company, what they did, the numbers and result; only what the transcript says)
- Synthesizer Mastermind required either 10,000 subscribers or $10,000 monthly income. When launched to a Synthesizer audience of about 15,000, the presenters realized 95% to 98% of that audience did not meet the requirement. They had to find prospects elsewhere to fill the program. They use this as a warning against setting a threshold too high.
- A member changed her content examples to business owners earning $10,000 who wanted $20,000, or $30,000 aiming for $50,000. She said new paid-community members began arriving in those categories. The presenters describe this as a “quiet barrier” created by messaging rather than a written rule.
- A member serving women entrepreneurs who had tried multiple approaches but remained stuck asked whether to use a specific sales threshold or a broader transformation criterion. The advice was to use the “achieved a valuable outcome” standard that Scaling uses, then refine after launching.
- Lucas had previously disliked high-ticket work because an earlier business had drained him. The coach sold him on resolving uncertainty about what to sell and building the right systems, rather than promising high ticket. They found his primary bottleneck was the product he fulfilled. He later reported $50,000 in the last seven days, though the transcript's timing and attribution details are not further specified.
- A strength-training consultant had spent over 10 years building recognition in a specialized methodology and estimated the market at under 5,000 studios, with about 800 contacts from a network audit. The presenters advised serving that niche deeply before testing adjacent markets. They cited another entrepreneur who thought a niche was saturated after making about $80 million, illustrating that market exhaustion can take far longer than expected.
## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not move from serving beginners to an extremely high barrier without checking how much of the audience remains eligible. Do not set qualification only to avoid difficult delivery if a lower bar still allows useful results.
- Do not let a niche label, a follower count, or a revenue number replace evidence that the prospect can benefit. The program's “valuable outcome” standard is offered as a practical alternative, not an invitation to invent arbitrary qualifications.
- Do not open marketing with “high ticket” when the audience associates it with something negative. Sell the desired outcome and explain the business model when appropriate.
- Do not mistake low-ticket for easier operations. The call notes that more customers can mean more questions, support, and churn, and says low-ticket communities may need about 10 times the traffic of high-ticket offers.
- Do not keep adding calls and content until members stop using the program. Product usage is described as a leading indicator of churn; too much unused access can remind members that they are paying for something they do not consume.
- Do not respond to exhaustion by abandoning a viable offer for a different model before testing group delivery. Improve the existing delivery mechanism first.
- Do not keep exploring adjacent markets before the current niche is served thoroughly. Do not make a one-to-many webinar the next step without enough sales repetitions and traffic.
## Numbers and benchmarks (conversion rates, call counts, prices, targets, time frames, with the context for each)
- The failed Mastermind threshold was 10,000 subscribers or $10,000 monthly income; 95% to 98% of the 15,000-person Synthesizer audience did not qualify.
- The presenters describe raising entry requirements over roughly 12 months to two years as a common response to frustration with beginner customers, but caution against setting the new line too high.
- The niche example estimated fewer than 5,000 studios and about 800 contacts; the recommendation was to exhaust the current market before adjacent expansion. The cited entrepreneur made about $80 million before believing their initial market had been saturated.
- One-to-one feedback for 60 Scaling members took about one to two full days. The subsequent delivery model included a weekly call and community/DM support.
- Scaling's first example of a group engagement event, High Ticket Synthesizer, had a Zoom capacity of 500 and about 700 people trying to join, according to the speaker.
- A low-ticket community was said to need roughly 10 times the traffic of a high-ticket model; this was a broad comparison, not a calculated benchmark for every offer.
- The call notes that webinars rarely sell above about $5,000 in the presenters' experience, and suggests considering webinars only after reaching a few hundred thousand dollars per month and needing greater scale. It says webinars are harder because the seller must handle objections one-to-many.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use when a user is unsure how narrowly to qualify prospects, attracts the wrong people, has an offer that prospects resist, or is moving from one-to-one delivery into a cohort. Ask what outcome the offer creates, what results the user has achieved, who has already benefited, which clients are difficult to serve, how many prospects meet the proposed requirement, whether delivery is one-to-one or group-based, and what public language and testimonials currently signal about the audience. Ask whether the current niche has been fully served before discussing adjacent markets.
