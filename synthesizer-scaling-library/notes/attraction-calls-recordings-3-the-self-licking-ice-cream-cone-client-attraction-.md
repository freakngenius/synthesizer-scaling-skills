# The Self Licking Ice Cream Cone (Client Attraction Call)
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: runtime not stated; transcript and attached notes, 1,825 lines
## One-paragraph summary
The call explains how a free Skool community can move new members toward booking calls, how to review the whole onboarding path from a member's perspective, and how to turn work already happening into content. It also reinforces the program's one-source rule, recommends tracking which channels produce customers rather than only members, and answers questions about community structure, cohort delivery, prospect follow-up, and improving the product. The host describes several practices used inside Synthesizer, while some systems discussed, especially automated win sharing, are still experiments rather than established results.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **One-source rule.** Implement one source's model before adding another; competing advice consumes execution time. The host makes a narrow exception for Sam Ovens's older YouTube whiteboard videos, which he says align with Synthesizer. He describes broad guru content as harder to apply across different business types and stages.
- **Free-to-paid Skool model.** Treat the free community as the lead magnet and use several visible and direct conversion paths to book a call, then sell into a paid group or offer. The listed paths include an onboarding VSL, direct messages to new members, a one-on-one onboarding call, and a weekly value webinar with a soft pitch. The speaker initially says there are four conversion points but remembers only three. The community's posts and conversations act like the nurture sequence in a traditional funnel.
- **Two appointment-setting flows.** For a free community, handle (1) new members through onboarding, welcome messages and follow-up; and (2) existing members through posts, engagement, two-step posts, and outreach to people who respond. A two-step post asks people to comment for a bonus or resource, creating a reason to start a conversation.
- **Member-journey review.** Enter the group as a new member and inspect the complete path: About page, membership questions, join experience, pinned posts, welcome or onboarding video, calendar, right-side links, and paid offer page. Screenshot the sequence and review whether the paid offer is visible and whether the message stays consistent. The principle is to locate gaps across the entire path, not optimize isolated assets.
- **Self-licking ice cream cone.** Adapt the phrase “self-licking ice cream cone” (the transcript's obvious mishearing of “self-licking”) to mean converting existing business activity into content. Map what already happens in the customer journey, such as comments, calls, workshops, testimonials, and client wins; select useful moments; get permission where needed; then repurpose them into posts, clips, or proof. This reduces dependence on inventing new content ideas. The call's proposed automation and publishing systems for sharing member wins are exploratory, not confirmed implementations.
- **GPS: Goal, Problem, Solution.** Identify the goal, name the main problem preventing it, and focus effort on the solution to that problem. Apply the same sequence to business bottlenecks and to customer delivery. The host says not to ask vaguely what to do next; ask what problem, if solved, will help the business scale.
- **Product improvement through problem removal.** Find where customers get stuck, then remove friction or provide a targeted over-deliver. Examples from the call include reviewing a client's Backbone when people struggle with it, or writing launch posts when that is the sticking point. The product gets better by helping customers reach outcomes more quickly and easily, rather than by adding features for their own sake.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Keep one pinned post with a clear call to action for the paid offer. The host says new members predominantly see pinned posts first. Use a welcome/onboarding video to introduce the offer and direct viewers to book a call; place the VSL in the onboarding video and as the first classroom video. A multi-video VSL can work, but the speaker suggests combining a five-video sequence into one video so more members reach the CTA.
2. Set an auto-DM to arrive within about two minutes of joining, if the platform setup allows. Use it to welcome the member and begin a conversation; the host's team does not link to a generic welcome post in its first DM. If volume is low, manually inspect new members and text or call them instead of buying automation.
3. Use membership questions to qualify and collect contact details. Synthesizer asks where the member is in their Skool journey, then collects email and phone number. In its advanced setup, a Zapier workflow sends a Slack alert when a respondent reports $1,000-$10,000 or $10,000+ income, so a setter can contact them quickly. This routing is optional and not needed at the start. Mark phone as optional if concerned about friction; the host says people who do not want to provide it can skip it.
4. Consider a calendar event with a call-booking link and description. During a launch, the team used a repeating 24-hour event, 11 a.m. to 11 a.m., which appeared as a prominent red banner. It ran for around seven days and was used once every couple of months. Email reminders every 24 hours can increase visibility but may annoy people if used continuously. Paid members may also see these, so use them less often and for shorter periods in paid groups.
5. Add a booking link through Skool's community links plugin: Settings, Community, Settings, Plugins, Links. The group description itself does not support a hyperlink, according to the call.
6. Repeat the same audience and outcome across the About page, questions, posts, videos, paid offer, and booking page. A mismatch can stop prospects from advancing.
7. For a periodic free challenge, the simple cadence offered was once every three months. The speaker also says to watch engagement and energy: run another when the right audience goes quiet and more calls are needed, then use observed intervals to set a better cadence. A member reported roughly $30,000 from a 21-day challenge, though the call does not specify the measurement period or attribution method.
8. Ask every new customer or lead where they came from, then compare member acquisition sources with paid-customer sources. In the example, 1,400 members joined through Skool discovery in the last 30 days, but the host's customer feedback pointed more strongly to Skool networking. Do not assume the channel with the most members produces the most buyers.
9. For a cold list, start with an introduction, proof, the specific audience and outcome, and a simple question or offer to reply “yes.” Invite respondents into the free group, continue the conversation, and work toward a call. Track each person in a spreadsheet with columns such as name, emailed, texted, called, follow-up, replied, and booked; move each lead through the stages and follow up when conversations stall.
10. During the first cohort, build next week's training while members use the current material. The initial program took about three months to build before sales reopened; later cohorts were easier because the training existed.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Host, new-member outreach:** “Hey, name, my name is Alexander. I help pet groomers start and scale [business/outcome]. I've achieved [proof], and I've just created a free community that has a 12 step process that walks through how you can get started for free. Reply, yes, if you're interested.” Use as a cold email or text template after replacing audience, proof, outcome, and resource with verified details.
- **Host, two-step post:** “Comment X, if you want Y bonus.” Use for an existing community post that invites an engagement-triggered follow-up.
- **Host, disengaged paid member:** Keep the message brief and move the conversation to a call: acknowledge the concern and invite them to discuss their situation. On the call, revisit why they joined and let them decide whether to continue.
- **Host, tracking question:** “Where did your paying clients actually come from?” Use alongside, not instead of, source tracking for new members.
## Examples and case studies (member or company, what they did, the numbers and result; only what the transcript says)
- A Synthesizer member ran a 21-day free challenge from existing material and reported about $30,000. The member said it re-engaged inactive people who fit the ideal customer profile; delivery took energy away from paid-client work. The host suggested starting with a quarterly cadence and adjusting based on engagement and capacity.
- The paid Scaling group had 791 win posts unknown to free Synthesizer members. The team considered member reposts, “This week in Scaling” roundups, AI monitoring, Skool Post Scout exports, and a testimonial website; these were experiments, not proven outcomes.
- A Skool Games attendee reportedly made $80,000 per month and booked calls by texting from his phone, an example of manual outreach working without specialized software.
## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not let new members pass through the free group without learning that a paid offer exists. The speaker says some groups have nearly all members join but only about 2% realize an offer is available.
- Do not make channel decisions from member counts alone; buyers may come from a different source.
- Do not buy messaging software before you have volume. Sendblue/iMessage was estimated at about $800 monthly; manual calls and texts are the suggested starting point.
- Do not leave a 24-hour event and repeated email reminder running indefinitely without considering fatigue or the value members receive.
- Do not treat a content-repurposing idea or AI automation as proven before testing it, securing permission, and confirming privacy preferences.
- Do not keep a customer who remains unhappy after a sales conversation just to preserve one payment. The host says the team will refund and move on when the customer still wants to leave, while acknowledging the decision can be harder for an early-stage business.
- Do not try to innovate on attract and convert systems before executing the program's proven process. Put experimentation into product delivery instead.
## Numbers and benchmarks (conversion rates, call counts, prices, targets, time frames, with the context for each)
- New-member attention: the speaker estimates reachability at 100% immediately, about 80% after a day, and about 50% after a week. These are illustrative estimates, not measured cohort data.
- Auto-DM timing: about two minutes after joining.
- Calendar event: 24-hour recurring window, 11 a.m. to 11 a.m.; run about seven days per launch, once every couple of months in the example.
- Cohort separation: one-on-one coaching with a few customers can stay in one group; the speaker says a second group makes more sense above five customers, especially for cohorts, and says to create a second group once above ten customers. These are contextual rules of thumb, not a single hard threshold.
- Free versus low-ticket conversion: the call gives approximate averages of 20% for a free community and 4% for a low-ticket community. The speaker uses this as a reason to consider free acquisition plus high-ticket sales; no source or calculation details are provided.
- Traffic: 1,400 free members from Skool discovery over the preceding 30 days; paid-client attribution was described as favoring networking.
- Delivery cadence: the example's first program build took about three months; quarterly challenges are a suggested starting cadence.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this call when a coach or educator has a free Skool group but too few new members book calls, when onboarding is fragmented, when they need low-effort content ideas, or when they are choosing between free and low-ticket entry. Ask what the offer is, how many members join weekly, which details they collect, what new members see first, what channel brought in paying clients, how much time they can give to follow-up, and whether customer wins may be shared publicly. For cohort advice, ask whether the program content already exists and where members currently wait or fall behind.
