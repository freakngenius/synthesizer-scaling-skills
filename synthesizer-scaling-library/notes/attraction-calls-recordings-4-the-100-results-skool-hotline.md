# The 100 Results + Skool Hotline
Type: call   Stage: Attract   Length/size: About 2 hours 10 minutes (stated in transcript)
## One-paragraph summary
The call announces results from a 90-day competition for 100 Skool owners, then pilots a live “Skool hotline” where members state their offer, revenue, goal, and main obstacle for coaching. The results discussion emphasizes the effect of peer competition, a wanted offer for a specific market, and focused attention on traffic and sales. The hotline coaching repeatedly directs members to solve the current bottleneck, increase inputs when a channel is working, choose a clear audience and result, and avoid building solutions for problems that have not appeared. The session also gives detailed rules for qualifying sales calls, handling DMs, hiring setters, and positioning cohort support.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- Competition identity and peer environment: A competition can raise action through peer examples and shared identity. Results were attributed to activity and environment, not a “magic pill.”
- Offer-market fit from the 100: Strong participants served a narrow audience with a wanted outcome grounded in expertise or experience: business owners growing, Latinos investing, artists selling work, or students passing exams. Specificity made content and sales clearer.
- Volume plus monetization: Pair traffic with an offer and focus on conversion. The speakers said every top-ten participant had a high-ticket offer; traffic, sales, and offer-market fit worked together.
- Solve the constraint in front of you: Increase the input that already works until a constraint appears. “Move fast and break things” means do not spend time on imagined problems. A full calendar may require sales capacity; heavy delivery becomes a problem to solve when it occurs.
- Four-part offer-choice framework: Compare (1) audience or network fit, (2) likely price, (3) enjoyment and skill growth, and (4) impact. The first two estimate revenue; the others inform motivation. If unclear, test both, then choose when evidence makes the answer clear.
- Clear, consistent positioning: Keep one niche and outcome consistent. Frequent changes to the offer or about page confuse prospects. Repeat the association between the creator and the result, like a recurring commercial.
- BANT qualification: Check budget, authority, need, and time. Filter people who lack funds, decision power, a strong need, or time to act.
- Founder-led conversion before delegation: The founder knows the niche. Establish and document the sales process, then train a setter to run it; do not delegate an unproven process.
- Cohort offer framing: Sell the transformation and path before delivery details. Explain the community, feedback, calls, and action steps if prospects assume a cohort means an unsupported course. Position one-to-one as premium continuity, not a prerequisite for cohort success.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Calculate competition growth as revenue during the 90 days minus the prior 90-day baseline. Validate receipts, invoices, and processed payments. Allow 48 hours after preliminary results for corrections, including currency errors.
2. For the hotline, collect name, audience and outcome, current and target revenue, and main obstacle. Select members, pin them on screen, then ask follow-ups to find the constraint. Brevity helps others understand. Anonymity option: change voice and blur face before publishing.
3. Check whether current sales can fill capacity before improving delivery. Jessie was preparing delivery for 20 to 30 cohort members without enough audience awareness; the advice was to attract and sell first.
4. If ads produce revenue, increase spend until a constraint appears. Seque reported $16,000 ad spend and $163,000 revenue over six months; advice was to scale, then address issues such as creative volume or funnel performance. Skool's $200,000/day spend was an illustration, not a member budget.
5. Follow Backbone, attraction, conversion, delivery. Start one-to-one, then scale delivery after demand. For a trading-card business, the first 30-day task was 10 offers; the operator owns business development while the expert supplies expertise.
6. Compare two offers using audience, price, enjoyment, and impact; test both if uncertain. One member was advised to sign a smaller client deal and free time rather than wait for a larger complex contract.
7. For a low close rate, measure call volume and qualification first. Add one budget question to Calendly or the application, confirm and qualify before the call, and clarify a “no” before cancelling. Cancel only when the prospect clearly cannot benefit or buy.
8. Notice hesitation during the pitch. After each pillar, ask what stands out, whether it helps, and what questions remain. Use repeated “temp checks.” Surface current expenses early; one prospect was paying $2,000/month for an agency with poor ROI, so cancellation was discussed before the new offer.
9. If objections repeat, check qualification, offer, and earlier discovery. Tie the promise to tangible business results, such as new clients or revenue, and study a strong competitor's sales process.
10. Hire a setter when leads can keep them busy, setting constrains the founder, and revenue supports pay. Suggested volume: 40 to 60 new opportunities or 20 to 40 qualified leads daily. Pay capacity: $1,000 to $3,000/month initially, $3,000 to $6,000 for a strong full-time setter. Give them a proven process.
11. Do not coach unqualified DMs. For qualified prospects, acknowledge the question, ask permission to learn more, qualify, then book. A brief answer may be followed by a discovery question; keep the conversation on booking.
12. If broad content brings traffic but few buyers, add business-outcome content rather than replacing what works. Test monetization posts a few times weekly and ask DMs whether the person has monetized or wants to start.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- Hotline participant: “My Skool helps [audience] [outcome]. I make [amount] a month. I'd like to make [amount], but what's stopping me is [obstacle].” Use as a concise submission; omit details that do not explain the constraint.
- Coach, on a hypothetical constraint: “If you don't know what's breaking, you just need to pump more volume until something breaks.”
- Coach, DM discovery: “Great question. I might ask you a question about that to see how I can help.” Use instead of giving a full consultation in chat.
- Coach, after qualification: “I definitely think I can help you. I would love to break down how we can apply this to your situation. Are you free Tuesday or Thursday?” Then send the calendar link.
- Coach, after a pitch pillar: “Does that make sense? What stands out to you? Do you think that will help you get closer to your goal?” Then ask what questions remain.
- Coach, light DM question: “Investing is the best idea ever. Have you started, or are you looking to get started?” Give a brief answer, then return to discovery.
- Sewing creator DM: “Have you already monetized your sewing, or are you looking to get started?” Use to identify buying intent.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not treat a 90-day rank as long-term potential; starting assets and life circumstances differed.
- Do not solve future delivery issues before demand creates them; act on the current constraint.
- Do not change positioning so often that prospects cannot connect the creator with a result.
- When time is limited, filter calls while preserving access to qualified prospects.
- Do not treat objections as a late-stage script issue; check fit, qualification, ROI, and missed hesitation.
- Do not turn DMs into free consulting; retain the advisor frame.
- Do not wait for testimonials when the offer has already sold without them.
- Do not present one-to-one as necessary if a clear cohort path can deliver the transformation.
