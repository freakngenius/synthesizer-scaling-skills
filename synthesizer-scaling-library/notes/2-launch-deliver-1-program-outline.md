# Program Outline Training
Type: lesson   Stage: Launch Deliver   Length/size: 31:54 video transcript plus worksheet text
## One-paragraph summary
A program is a guided sequence built to produce a transformation, not a large collection of information. The outline makes the client journey visible as a small set of ordered phases, drawn from the creator's own backstory and pitch. For this lesson, create only the phases: use the existing Business Backbone and pitch, identify 3 to 6 milestones, name them simply, and stop. Modules and action steps come later, as clients progress. Keep the first version short, test it, and remove anything that is not needed for the promised result.

## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)

### Program versus course
A course is presented as a collection of information that the learner must sift, organize, and apply alone. A program is a guided journey, arranged logically to get a result. A program can be delivered one-to-one, one-to-many, as a cohort, or through training. Its defining feature is the sequence that guides someone to a transformation. More information does not automatically increase value; excessive information can increase overwhelm and reduce action.

### Program hierarchy
The hierarchy has three connected levels:
1. **Phases:** key milestones the participant must reach, in order, to achieve the outcome. These are the main containers of the program.
2. **Modules:** only the knowledge needed to take action and move through a phase. Reference material that does not support action is a distraction.
3. **Actions:** the concrete steps the participant takes using the knowledge in a module. Actions produce progress and results.

Phases without modules leave people unclear about how to advance. Modules without actions give information without driving progress. The dependencies run from phases to modules to actions.

### Less is more, and delete before adding
Keep only what a participant needs to achieve the transformation. Test each proposed phase by asking: "What can I remove and the person will still be able to get the transformation?" Omit material that is interesting but not necessary. If participants later get stuck at a particular point, add the missing support then. The instructor calls this improving the value-to-time density: remove the gravel and keep the useful material.

### Outline-to-delivery sequence
The recommended order is:
1. Define the phases from the creator's backstory and pitch pillars.
2. Use the phases to make the welcome/onboarding video, so new clients understand the journey.
3. After selling and beginning delivery, build modules as needed.
4. Derive action items while creating each module.

This sequencing is intended to avoid months of building a complete course before validating it with clients.

### Synthesizer Scaling example
The program's broad philosophy is summarized as Attract, Convert, Deliver. The more detailed roadmap is presented as five core phases, with bonus training as an optional sixth: Business Backbone, Rapid Launch Protocol, Attraction Systems, Conversion Systems, Delivery Systems, and Bonus Trainings. The first phase clarifies business fundamentals such as niche and offer. Rapid Launch proves the offer by getting the first 1 to 5 sales from a warm audience and network. The next phases establish attraction, conversion, and scalable delivery. Bonus trainings address roadblocks as they arise.

## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Open the Business Backbone document already completed, including the backstory, rough deliverables, and pitch.
2. Open the Program Outline Worksheet and use File > Make a copy. Rename the copy for the program or creator. This is an internal working document, not a client-facing asset.
3. Extract 3 to 6 milestones from the creator's own path to the outcome. Use pitch pillars as prompts and a starting point, not as a requirement to preserve every point.
4. Ask in sequence: What first fundamental quick win did I need before the transformation? What was the next important problem I had to solve? What next ramped up progress? What further milestone was needed to reach the outcome?
5. Write a short, descriptive name for each phase. In the worksheet, the phase names can be accompanied by a brief description of the milestone or result.
6. Set a 20-minute timer and aim to finish a first draft in that time. If the work takes longer, continue until it is done. Three to six phases should be a manageable first pass, and six is the stated upper limit.
7. Leave the module and action columns alone for now. Return to them only when the program reaches the stage where those details are needed.
8. Use the outline to make the welcome video in the next lesson, then begin testing and improving the offer and pitch against the clarified journey.
9. Ask for help in implementation calls or the community if stuck. The instructor recommends posting the draft for support rather than attempting to perfect it in isolation.

The outline should make it possible to see how the creator gets clients results at a glance. It is not intended as a polished client deliverable. Build from what is currently known and accept that the phases can change as the program is tested.

## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Creator, while extracting phases:** "What was the first fundamental quick win I needed to have/do in order to be ready for the transformation?" Use this to find the participant's first milestone.
- **Creator, while ordering milestones:** "What was the most important problem I needed to solve/achieve to make progress?" Then ask, "What was the next thing that ramped things up?" Use these prompts to keep the roadmap sequential.
- **Creator, while editing:** "What can I remove and the person will still be able to get the transformation?" Apply this to every phase or proposed lesson.
- **Worksheet format:** `[Name's] Program Outline`, followed by Phase, Modules, and Action columns. Phase prompts include `[Fundamental quick win they need first]`, `[most important milestone to make progress]`, and `[the core milestone that ramped things up]`. Fill phase names and descriptions first; do not fill in the later columns during this assignment.
- **Synthesizer example descriptions:** Business Backbone: "Crystal clear on business fundamentals (niche, offer, etc.)" Rapid Launch Protocol: "Prove your offer by getting the first 1-5 sales." Attraction Systems: "Dream clients know who you are. Tons of eyeballs." Conversion Systems: "Consistently converting eyeballs into clients." Delivery Systems: "Deliver results to clients at scale." Bonus Trainings: "Remove roadblocks as they come up."

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not equate a program with a course or try to increase value by stuffing in more information.
- Do not build every module and action before the phases are clear. The current assignment is phases only.
- Do not treat the worksheet as a public-facing client document.
- Do not keep content merely because it is interesting or was part of the creator's experience. Retain it only if participants need it for the transformation.
- Do not wait for certainty or spend months polishing. The phases can change as the creator observes clients using the program.
- Do not go above six phases in the first outline. Synthesizer has five core phases; the bonus training category is treated as optional.
- Do not confuse time spent planning with mastery. The instructor says mastery comes from working people through the process and improving from what happens.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use this when an educator, coach, or consultant has a transformation to deliver but their program feels sprawling, is still only a pile of information, or seems to require months of prebuilding. Before applying it, ask what outcome the client is meant to achieve, what the creator has already done to achieve it, whether a Business Backbone/backstory and pitch exist, and which steps in that story were necessary rather than incidental. Also ask whether the immediate goal is only an initial phase outline. Keep the exercise limited to 3 to 6 named phases and defer module design until needed.
