# 2.1 Launch Prep Call + Worksheet
Type: call   Stage: Launch Prep   Length/size: approximately 1:54:14 video transcript plus worksheet
## One-paragraph summary
The call explains how to launch first to people already in a creator's network and audience, test an offer before committing to it, and then use the launch to guide longer-term audience growth. It covers four phases, prep, attract, convert, and deliver, and two launch schedules: 21 days for smaller networks and one-to-one coaching, 60 days for larger audiences and cohorts. Prep means auditing existing opportunities, testing offer-message resonance with posts and conversations, and warming people. The accompanying worksheet sets dates, collects links, and lists the week's actions. Q&A addresses audience size, call scheduling, premium-price cues, delivery capacity, community setup, and people already partway through a launch.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- **Rapid Launch Protocol:** Prep maps opportunities, tests resonance, and warms people. Attract promotes the launch and books calls. Convert holds sales calls and enrolls customers. Deliver provides the promised result. This short-term sequence maximizes existing opportunities before the broader scaling roadmap builds new audience and opportunities.
- **Opportunity audit:** The network audit inventories individuals and contacts, including previous clients, work contacts, social followers, email contacts, and community members. The audience audit maps channels and groups where relevant attention can be reached, such as YouTube, Skool, email, Instagram, and LinkedIn. Sort by warmth and accessibility.
- **Message resonance:** Test whether people respond to the offer and desired outcome. Use posts, direct chats, and optional research calls, then revise the Backbone worksheet. Do not assume a Google-doc offer is validated. In prep, test multiple ideas with tentative wording. In attract, commit to the launch and date.
- **21-day launch:** Recommended when expected sales volume is small and delivery is one-to-one. Week 1: prep. Week 2: attract, focused on booking calls, with sales calls held back. Week 3: convert while continuing to attract. Week 4 onward: deliver while one-to-one attracting and converting can continue.
- **60-day launch:** Recommended for a larger audience and cohort delivery. Use one or two weeks for prep, one for attract, then about four weeks of calls and conversion while still attracting. When the cohort is full, stop selling and focus on delivery. Adjust the schedule to the available opportunities and capacity.
- **Warm-to-cold sequence:** Start with the warmest, most accessible people; move to less active contacts and broader channels only as needed. Results show which channels to emphasize later.
- **Free community / paid classroom:** Sell access through a paid classroom course and unlock paid group calls in the calendar for buyers. A separate group can add unnecessary complexity at small scale. At about 20+ clients, separate space may help retain personalized feedback. A one-way Telegram channel can deliver logistics to paid participants without posting to a free or low-ticket group.
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Set calendar start and end dates for prep, attract, convert, and deliver. Do not wait until you feel ready; use the prep phase to clarify the offer.
2. Complete the network and audience audits. Identify people, groups, platforms, and lists you can reach. Start with the warmest contacts and strongest access.
3. Test the message in prep. The worksheet asks for three launch-prep posts, outreach to 30+ people, and 5+ research calls. Research calls are optional in the worksheet but recommended in the call.
4. On research calls, ask about goals, desires, problems, and current work. Do not pitch. Use what you hear to refine the offer and warm people for later sales calls. Calls were estimated at about 30 minutes; a 15-minute discovery version was suggested for already-booked appointments.
5. In prep, say you are thinking about creating something, without saying the launch is certain. In attract, announce what is happening and when, and direct people to book. Open sales-call calendar slots for the conversion period. You can close or limit the calendar during prep, but do not cancel calls already booked.
6. Separate attracting from sales calls when doing both would overwhelm you or leave no time to book more calls. A focused attract week can prefill the calendar. If you can manage both, combining them is allowed. Consider opening slots several weeks ahead so calls cluster together.
7. Signal a premium price before calls without necessarily stating the amount. Mention limited places and commitment, and add a calendar/application question that indicates the offer will be expensive. This filters prospects who expect a very low price.
8. For mixed-skill cohorts, enroll people whose needs fit the same program and sequence. People may move through the program at different speeds. Avoid positioning that makes qualified people think they are not ready.
9. If already attracting or holding a waitlist, choose the date to start converting and work backward. Existing calls may be sold, repurposed as research and rebooked, or sold early with a clear future start date. Preserve a flow that is working rather than copying the schedule mechanically.
10. Give early cohort buyers an onboarding video or first resource if they must wait weeks for the cohort start. Gate paid calls through the paid classroom course settings.
11. Workload estimates: training about one hour; audience audit three minutes; network audit one to three hours; each prep post about 30 minutes; outreach about one hour a day; research calls about 30 minutes each. Do what available time allows.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- **Coach, prep whisper post:** “Things feel different. The tides are shifting.” Use as a deliberately vague public commitment that something new is coming. The speaker said the wording is not optimized copy; the point is to signal change and invite curiosity. An emoji-only post is also fine.
- **Coach, prep offer test:** “I'm thinking about creating an offer to help [niche] achieve [outcome].” Use to test a segment or result without committing. Another prompt: “If I could help you achieve one result, what would you want my help with?”
- **Coach, proof-based post:** “I've [achieved/helped someone achieve result]. If I were to coach you, what would you want my help with?” Use to learn which outcomes people want.
- **Coach, demand test:** “I'm thinking of helping five YouTubers to grow their YouTube channel. Comment if interested.” Adapt audience and result to the offer.
- **Coach, booking-page price cue:** “This will be expensive. Is that okay?” Use to set a premium expectation before a call; it is an example, not required wording.
- **Coach, research-call reset:** “I'm just doing this to make sure [the program] is really awesome. I'm going to ask you a few questions about what you need, what you're working on, what your goals are, and then at the end, if I think we're going to be a good fit, we'll get you rebooked into the official strategy call... There's no offer that's going to be made on today's call.” Use to explain a short discovery call and schedule a later sales conversation.
- **Coach, transition existing clients to cohort:** Explain the new cohort as a better or more valuable format, offer existing one-to-one clients a free transition in thanks for their prior investment, and say it replaces the old delivery model. If they object because they want individual help, offer on-demand one-to-one support as needed. The $5,800 cited was an example price for that cohort, not a general benchmark.
## Examples and case studies (member or company, what they did, the numbers and result; only what the transcript says)
- Prior launch results cited by the presenter: Aiden went from $0 to $10,000/month in three months without an audience; Steve Ho made $165,000 in 10 days; Brandon made $200,000 in seven days; Hamza went from $20,000 to $200,000 in 10 days; Jenny Coyos made $36,000; Adam made $50,000; Leon Hendris made $120,000 in 10 days; Synthesizer made $350,000. These are examples, not promised results.
- The presenter said he made roughly 55 to 60 research calls in three days after joining Synthesizer, without selling, to learn people's needs, problems, and desires.
- For Synthesizer's launch, the team started with active community members, then expanded to broader posts and channels. It had about 30,000 Twitter followers, YouTube, email, and 3,000 to 6,000 phone numbers available, but said warmer opportunities filled the cohort. The team selected 60 places after assessing capacity.
- A participant with nine one-to-one clients considered adding a cohort. Advice: transition existing clients instead of running both delivery models in parallel. The presenter reported that 99 out of 100 accepted his own transition offer; this is anecdotal.
- A member had five clients on three-month timelines, purchased at different times. They could run another cohort alongside existing delivery or wait about six weeks. Clear action steps for everyone were the main requirement.
## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not begin with cold prospects while warm contacts, previous clients, communities, and channels remain untested.
- Do not treat a written offer as validated. Talk with people and revise based on their responses.
- Do not pitch on calls designated as research. Set expectations and rebook sales-ready prospects.
- Do not leave launch dates blank until you feel ready. Do not cancel existing calls just because you are reorganizing the calendar.
- Avoid combining attraction and conversion if full calendars prevent ongoing booking. A calendar that fills without more leads can leave you scrambling after calls end.
- Do not surprise low-price buyers with an unanticipated premium price. Hint at price level and qualify applicants.
- Avoid running one-to-one and cohort delivery in parallel if it creates multiple support streams. Do not add a second community without a delivery need.
- During a launch, keep value posts related to the offer and focus on one message at a time.
- Do not delay selling to build a complete course. Sell, learn what buyers need, and build or revise the training as you go.
- A launch can produce zero sales. The message, offer, resonance, or reach may be off; revise and continue rather than treating one result as a verdict on the business.
## Numbers and benchmarks (conversion rates, call counts, prices, targets, time frames, with the context for each)
- 21-day plan: prep week 1; attract week 2; convert plus attract week 3; delivery from week 4 onward.
- 60-day plan: one to two prep weeks; one attract week; about four weeks of conversion while attracting; then delivery.
- Expected customers guide: fewer than five suggests one-to-one; more than 10 suggests cohort; five to 10 is a choice. These are guidelines, not hard cutoffs.
- One-to-one capacity: start with a cap of five if uncertain; a more confident operator might take 10. The speaker said the system starts to break around 20 one-to-one clients, though group transition can happen at five or six.
- Worksheet targets: three prep posts, 30+ outreach contacts, and 5+ research calls. Work estimates are listed under tactics.
- Pricing examples from Q&A: $97/month versus a recommended $1,000 upfront option with monthly as a possible downsell; a $5,000 pitch-deck project followed by a $1,000/month retainer. These are individual examples, not general prices.
- Extreme workload examples: sales calls from 7 a.m. to 9 p.m.; 24 to 28 hours of delivery across the final three days. These were not presented as normal expectations.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use when an educator, coach, consultant, or service provider has a potential offer but has not tested it or organized existing contacts into a launch. Ask what warm contacts and channels they can reach, expected client volume, one-to-one versus cohort delivery, service capacity, available weekly time, and the date they can take sales calls. Also ask whether they already have a waitlist or booked calls, what evidence supports the offer, and what price prospects expect. For someone mid-launch, identify what is already working before changing the plan.
