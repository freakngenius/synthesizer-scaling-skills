# Pitch Training
Type: lesson   Stage: Launch Convert   Length/size: 32:14 video transcript plus training notes
## One-paragraph summary
This lesson teaches the pitch as the bridge between a prospect's current situation and desired result. Keep it to 2-5 minutes and make the path logical, specific, and easy to follow. The Bridge Framework has three parts: a result-based promise of transformation, three short proof-backed pillars that progress in sequence, and a concise explanation of how the offer is delivered. The pitch should use the prospect's own concerns, demonstrate proof, and invite dialogue. The lesson instructs users to build the pitch from their Business Backbone, then improve it through practice rather than relying on slides or an elaborate presentation.
## Core ideas and frameworks (named concepts, each with its steps or components, as taught)
- Bridge Framework:
  1. Promise of Transformation: one sentence about the measurable result and timeframe, written around the prospect's desired state.
  2. Three Bridge Pillars: a logical sequence of steps that explains how the offer gets the prospect from current to desired state. Each is short, clear, supported by proof, and based on a mechanism rather than a cosmetic improvement.
  3. Delivery Across the Bridge: how the service/program is delivered, explained through benefits and support outcomes, without getting lost in feature detail.
- Three-pillar logic: Pillar 1 is the foundational quick win; Pillar 2 addresses the core problem through the offer's mechanism; Pillar 3 answers what comes next after the main problem is solved and helps the prospect continue toward the desired result.
- Business Backbone mapping: the Outcome becomes the Promise of Transformation; the Unique Mechanism informs the pitch pillars; Deliverables become the delivery explanation. The seller should customize the sequence and examples to the prospect's stated situation.
- Boolean result language: describe something tangible enough to assess as true or false, such as a stated number of pounds in a set period, rather than a vague promise to “get in shape.”
## Tactics and how-tos (concrete, ordered, include any numbers, thresholds, timings, tools, settings)
1. Keep the full pitch to 2-5 minutes. Do not turn it into a 15-20-minute presentation. Lead with one sentence describing the current situation, desired state, and timeframe.
2. Ask the prospect to write down the three pillars before explaining them. State the first pillar, connect it to something the prospect mentioned, explain the old approach and its drawback, then explain the new mechanism and its benefits. End with a check-in question.
3. Present the second pillar as the next logical move after the first. Explain the core problem it solves, state the mechanism and outcome, and ask what questions the prospect has.
4. Use the third pillar to explain the next need after initial success. Attach a relevant client result as evidence. Avoid promising that the example result will happen for every prospect unless evidence supports that claim.
5. Explain delivery before the prospect has to ask “how does this work?” Cover what they receive and how that support helps them get started, avoid getting lost, and continue progress. Translate features into benefits: for example, a one-on-one call is a feature; rapid access to support when a problem arises is the benefit.
6. Use clear, straightforward pillar names. The mechanism is distinctive because of how it works, not because it has an elaborate name. Keep the explanation understandable without a deck or screen share.
7. Keep the pitch conversational. Pause after each pillar to ask what stands out, whether it could help, or what questions the prospect has. Adjust the sequence and emphasis to match what the prospect said during discovery.
8. Attach proof to claims. The lesson's templates refer to prior client results or an average result; use only substantiated proof that actually applies to the offer.
9. Practice the pitch through repeated calls, role-play, and revision. The speaker says early pitches may take 10, 20, or 30 attempts to become confident and consistent.
## Scripts, templates and wording (verbatim short examples of messages, posts, hooks, questions; mark each with who says it and when to use it)
- Coach, transformation promise: “This is a 3-phase program to help you get from [current situation] to [desired situation] in about [timeframe].” Use as the opening sentence; fill with a verifiable result.
- Coach, start the pillars: “Would you mind writing down [1-3] in your notes? Just so we make sure we don’t miss anything.” Ask immediately before the pillar sequence.
- Coach, pillar 1: “The first pillar is called [name]. You see, after [proof] we’ve noticed that most people are doing [old way]... which leads to [problems], which results in [painful thing]. What we do instead is [pillar description], so you can [benefit] and [benefit]. What stands out to you about that?” Use for a foundational step and connect it to the prospect's issue.
- Coach, pillar 2: “Now that we have taken care of [pillar 1] you’ve already got [benefit] and are ready for our second phase, [pillar 2 name]. Here we’ve noticed after [proof] that most people need [description]. So that’s why we do [solution] so you can get [result]. What questions do you have about that?”
- Coach, pillar 3: “Now at this point in time you’re finally ready for [pillar 3]. Here, where we noticed people go wrong is once they have [pillar 1] and [pillar 2] set up they stop… This means they reach a ceiling they can’t get past. So what we do instead is [final mechanism]. This is what allowed one of my clients to [stop doing painful thing] and start [doing things they want], which ultimately resulted in [benefit]. Does that make sense?” Only use a true case result in the proof slot.
- Coach, delivery: “Now how this all works is first we will get you set up with [first deliverable]. This lets you get results right out of the gate. Now you also get [second deliverable] which means you will never get lost and will always have the [support] you need.” Use to answer how participation works, without listing every feature.
- Coach, dialogue: “What stands out to you about that?” or “What questions do you have about that pillar?” Ask after each section, not only at the end.

## Mistakes, warnings and "do not" rules
- Do not confuse the prospect with a long or complicated pitch. The lesson says confusion kills sales and recommends simple, short, easy-to-understand language.
- Do not use fancy mechanism names as a substitute for a distinct process. Use names that explain what the offer does.
- Do not rely on pitch slides. Be able to explain the offer without screen sharing.
- Do not focus on features instead of benefits and results. Explain what a deliverable enables the client to do.
- Do not turn the pitch into a monologue. Make it a conversation and check the prospect's response after pillars.
- Do not state a vague, non-tangible promise or make unsupported proof claims. The training calls for specific results, timeframes, and proof attached to each pillar.
- Do not deliver the same word-for-word pitch to everyone. Customize based on the prospect's current situation and stated goal, while preserving the offer's actual scope.

## Best used when (what kind of user or situation this helps, and what to ask the user before using it)
Use when a user has completed discovery and needs to explain a premium offer clearly in a sales call. Before drafting, ask for the prospect's exact current problem and desired result, offer outcome and timeframe, unique mechanism, three logical program steps, deliverables, and proof supporting every claim. Ask which parts of the offer the prospect already understands and what they said they want. Confirm any case-study figures and whether they apply to this offer before using them.
